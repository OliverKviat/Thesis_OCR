Abstract
Tremor affects everyone, and although in most cases it is physiological and not even
visible, in some cases, it has pathological causes and greatly disturbs those who suffer
from it. However, the way in which it is diagnosed and monitored relies on visual as­
sessment which is very subjective, and hard, especially in early stages of tremor. This
project aims to develop a reliable device for quantifying hand tremor, utilizing inertial sen­
sors and Arduino­based hardware. The proposed system captures tremor data using
3­axis accelerometers, processes the signals, and performs their analysis to compute
key indices that aid in the diagnosis and monitoring of tremors. To validate this device,
comparative measurements were performed using a calibrated sensor and the CATSYS
system. The results obtained demonstrate the great potential that this project has, as this
device appears to be a promising tool for assessing tremor in a clinical setting.

Chapter 1: Introduction
Everyone has tremor, whether it is visible or not. In most cases, it is physiological, and
so small that it isn’t visible to the naked eye. When pathological, it can have a number
of causes, such as the use of some medications or drugs, or specific diseases, as in
the cases of Parkinson’s Disease and Essential Tremor. Due to the variety of causes,
it’s important to achieve a correct diagnosis. However, that is no easy task. Not only is
this not a standardized exercise, but it is also typically dependent on a clinician’s visual
assessment, which can be highly subjective and hard at times, as tremor is not always
visible, especially in the early stages. Once a diagnosis is obtained, it is also important
to correctly monitor tremor, which is also dependent on visual assessment and subjective
measures.
This project aims to address these limitations by developing and implementing an objec­
tive method for diagnosing tremor. This will be achieved using Micro­Electro­Mechanical
Systems (MEMS) technology to measure tremor, together with software developed for its
analysis. The system is validated against a calibrated Brüel & Kjær (BK) sensor and the
CATSYS device, a system used for more than 10.000 tremor examinations since 2000.
1.1
Background and Motivation
1.1.1
Tremor
Tremor is an involuntary rhythmic movement of one or more body parts, and the most
common movement disorder [1, 2]. It can have many causes, being the most common
Parkinson’s Disease and Essential Tremor. Essential Tremor is the most common patho­
logical cause of tremor, affecting 0.4 to 6% of the population [1, 3]. It is usually present
from a young age, however, due to its long progression, most people do not seek a clini­
cian until the age of 60. Parkinson’s Disease is a degenerative pathology, in which there
is a loss of dopaminergic neurons in the brain. This causes not only tremor, but also
difficulty initiating movements, slowness, and muscle rigidity. This pathology affects ap­
proximately 0.11% of the population worldwide and has become more prevalent in recent
years. Of these, 70% are affected by tremor [4, 1, 5, 3].
Although tremor itself is not life­threatening, it causes a great disturbance in the everyday
life of those affected by it. It affects one’s ability to write, eat, drink, get dressed, and
perform any task that requires a steady hand. Depending on the severity of the tremor,
individuals may lose their independence completely. This emphasizes the importance
of obtaining the correct diagnosis and correctly monitoring it through time, so that the
appropriate course of treatment can be implemented to, at least, alleviate symptoms.
1.1.2
Challenges in the assessment of tremor
Assessment of tremor is a challenging task, primarily because it is not standardized and
there are no established biomarkers for it. The main goals are to characterize tremor in
terms of activating condition, topographic distribution, and frequency. This is usually done
through a physical examination, in which the physician asks the patient to perform a series
of tasks. During these, physicians can also rely on scales, such as the Unified Parkin­
son’s Disease Rating Scale or Essential Tremor Rating Assessment Scale, which help
classify tremor in a severity scale. However, these types of assessments depend on the
tremor being visible, which may not be the case in the early stages. More exams can be

performed, such as medical imaging, to exclude certain causes, like structural changes.
These can, however, become expensive while not leading to a specific diagnosis.
As can be seen, tremor assessment is a very subjective task. It varies from doctor to doc­
tor and depends on their experience, as well as the visibility of the tremor. This highlights
a significant need for an objective and standardized method to diagnose tremor and also
monitor its progression through time. Although some are being developed in industry,
they are, in most cases, specific for one pathology, which is usually Parkinson’s Disease.
This means that not only are they not approved for use in the diagnosis and monitoring of
multiple pathologies, but they may also not be suitable for this purpose. The Coordination

develop and validate a new CATSYS, a system that can be used in a clinical environment
for the diagnosis and monitoring of various tremor types caused by different conditions.

2.1
Tremor
Movement disorders are a group of neurological conditions that affect movement, either
by increasing or decreasing it [6]. Tremor is defined as an involuntary rhythmic movement
of one or more body parts and is the most common movement disorder [1, 2]. It usually
affects the hands but can extend to other body parts such as the arms, legs, head, vocal
cords, and torso [7]. Different types of tremor can be found and classified depending on
their characteristics or causes.
Depending on its characteristics, tremor can be classified as Resting or Action tremor,
and within Action, it can be Postural, Isometric, or Kinetic tremor [7].
• Resting tremor: When the person or body part is at rest and supported against
gravity.
• Action tremor: When there is a voluntary muscle contraction.
– Postural tremor: When holding a position against gravity, such as when the
patient stretches his arms in front of his chest.
– Kinetic tremor: When there is a voluntary movement.
* Intention tremor: When a movement is directed towards a target, such as
touching a specific point with the finger.
* Task­specific tremor: When performing goal­specific tasks, such as hand­
writing.
– Isometric tremor: When holding a stiff position that is not accompanied by any
further movement.
Based on their cause, there is also another classification of tremors [7, 1, 5].
• Enhanced physiologic tremor
• Essential tremor
• Parkinsonian tremor
• Drug­induced tremor
• Cerebellar tremor
• Psychogenic/functional tremor
• Dystonic tremor
• Wilson’s disease
• Rare tremors
– Orthostatic tremor
– Neuropathic tremor
– Holmes’ tremor

2.2
Assessment and Treatment of Tremor
In this section, technique for the assessment of tremor will be presented. That is, for both
its diagnosis and monitoring, which use some of the same techniques.
2.2.1
Clinical Assessment of Tremor
The diagnosis of tremor is still not a standardized exercise, however, it is important to
find the underlying cause to find the best treatment course for a patient. Usually, a doctor
will diagnose based on the patient’s and family history, as well as a physical examination.
During physical examination, which is crucial for tremor assessment, the first step is to
categorize the tremor with regard to the activation condition, the topographic distribution,
and the frequency [1].
Activation condition can be Resting, Action, or Isometric tremor, and their subtypes. This
is assessed by asking the patient to take different positions, such as sitting with the hands
on the lap or in a table, sitting with the arms extended to the front, or performing a finger­to­
nose test as seen in Figure 2.1. The topographic distribution relates to the affected area
of the body. Finally, frequency relates to the frequency of the tremor, which is usually
divided into three groups: low frequency (< 4 Hz), medium frequency (4−7 Hz), and high
frequency (> 7 Hz) [1].
In addition to the previously mentioned, further assessment can be made for signs associ­
ated with tremor syndromes. This includes searching for certain factors that can alleviate
or exacerbate the tremor. An example is caffeine intake and fatigue, which are often ex­
acerbating factors in ET, while small amounts of alcohol relieve it. For PD, other signs that
could be associated with tremor are bradykinesia, postural instability, rigidity, and masked
faces. If both PD and ET are possible diagnoses, then the patient may be asked to draw
an Archimedes spiral as seen in Figure 2.2. A large and shaky spiral would indicate that
the patient has ET, while a smaller spiral would mean PD [1].
Medical imaging can also be used to differentiate between some causes of tremor or rule
out some causes. For example, Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI) and CT scan can
be used to rule out causes, or when physical examination suggests a structural cause.
In cases of uncertainty, Single­Photon Emission Computed Tomography (SPECT) using
ioflupane can be used to assess dopamine transporter dysfunction, thereby leading to the
diagnosis of PD. However, clinical evaluation is still superior to this test [1].
Clinical scales can also be used to measure tremor and reach a possible diagnosis. The
Fahn­Tolosa­Marin (FTM) scale can be used for all types of tremor, the Essential Tremor

2.2.2
Electrophysiologic Measurement of Tremor
Electrophysiology is an extension of the clinical evaluation of tremor, and is most com­
monly used to quantify tremor amplitude and frequency. Tools to perform these kind of
measurements include motion transducers, surface Electromyography (EMG), Electroen­
cephalography (EEG), and Magnetoencephalography (MEG) [3].
Movements are easily recorded using motion transducers, such as Inertial Measurement
Unit (IMU), and the associated muscle activity can also be measured using an EMG. From
these measurements, the rhythmicity of tremor can be quantified using frequency domain
analysis. To have a rhythmic movement, there should be at least, a peak in the frequency
domain, with a narrow bandwidth. Another measure of rhythmicity is entropy, the more
rhythmic a movement is, the lower the entropy will be. This means that physiologic tremor
will have greater entropy than pathological tremor. The most common algorithms for cal­
culating entropy are Sample Entropy (SampEn) and Approximate Entropy (ApEn) [3].
EMG and motion transducers can also be used to detect tremor at rest. In a resting posi­
tion, there is always tremor, however, in a pathological case, this tremor is accompanied
with rhythmic EMG activity at the frequency of the tremor [3]. In addition, using motion
transducers, such as an Accelerometer (Acc), it’s also possible to quantify the amplitude
of tremor, which is typically smaller in healthy individuals and increases in the presence

2.2.3
Treatment of tremor
Most forms of tremor can not be treated, but in some cases, there’s the possibility to
manage the symptoms. When there’s a chance to treat underlying health conditions,
there’s a possibility that tremor can also be cured or reduced.
One of the ways to reduce tremor is by using medications. These include [7]:
• Beta­blockers: used mostly in patients with Action tremor
• Anti­seizure medications: in some cases, this type of medication can reduce tremor
in patients with ET who do not respond to beta­blockers
• Dopaminergic medications: usually used to treat tremors associated with PD

• Anticholinergic medications: can sometimes be used to treat Dystonic tremors
• Botulinum toxin (Botox): these injections can be useful to treat Dystonic head and
hand tremors
When medications are ineffective, surgery may be considered as a treatment option. The
most common form of surgical treatment for tremor is Deep Brain Stimulation (DBS). This
treatment is typically used for Parkinsonian Tremor, Essential Tremor, and Dystonia, and
involves the implantation of electrodes in the thalamus that send high­frequency electric
signals. This electrode is connected to a pulse­generating device that is placed under
the skin in the upper chest, which sends electrical stimuli to stop the tremor temporarily.
Radiofrequency ablation is a technique in which a radio wave is used to generate an
electric current that disrupts the nerves’ activity for six months or more. Another technique
is Focused ultrasound, where MRI is used to deliver focused ultrasounds that create small
lesions in the thalamus areas that are thought to be causing the tremor. This treatment
is only approved for patients who do not respond well to anti­seizure medication or beta­
blockers [7].
Finally, tremor can also be reduced by lifestyle changes. Physical, speech, and occupa­
tional therapy can help to control tremor and adapt to the daily challenges that it causes.
Reducing caffeine intake and avoiding stressful situations can also help, as well as getting
enough sleep, as all of these factors can aggravate tremors. Stopping certain medications
that can be triggering or aggravating tremor [7].
2.3
The CATSYS System
The Coordination Ability Test System (CATSYS) is a device that allows the study of co­
ordination, reaction time, tremor, and postural stability. This is achieved by the use of
four different sensors, a tremor pen, a touch recording plate (recording drum), a reaction
time handle, and a force plate, which can be seen in Figure 2.4. Since 2000, the Depart­
ment of Neurosurgery and Neurointervention of Semmelweis University in Budapest has
performed nearly 10.000 tremor examinations using this device.
Figure 2.4: Coordination Ability Test System (CATSYS)
During data collection, the appropriate sensor for the selected test will send the data to
the datalogger, where it is stored temporarily before being transmitted to the computer.
Afterwards, the results are presented in a Windows software [12]. The data logger is the
main part of this system. It has connections to the sensors and the computer, as well as
a serial port that can be used in cases where a USB connection is unavailable. It also
presents reset and volume buttons, as well as information diodes.
For this project, the sensor of interest is the tremor pen, which records tremor using ac­
celerometry. The accelerometer is placed at the tip of the pen and consists of a 2­axes

Barrantes et al. were able to use the accelerometer in an iPhone 5S to distinguish be­

used features in the frequency domain, such as the total spectral power, relative power
contribution to the first harmonic, and relative energy, in Rest and Postural tremor, to find
suitable thresholds to make those two types of discrimination. Also using ML, Bazgir et

scale [32]. They recorded tremor using an Accelerometer from a smartphone, and used
features in the time and frequency domain to classify tremor from 0 (non severe) to 4
(very severe). In [33], Abujrida et al. used a Multi­Layer 1D Convolutional Neural Net­
work to classify gait data acquired with a smartphone. The authors describe using a large
dataset, mPower, in which patients used a phone to record gait on their daily life, to clas­
sify the severity of PD symptoms. By pre­processing the data using a moving average
to smooth the signal, mean subtraction to remove DC shifts, and identifying gait cycles.
They achieved an accuracy of 98.4% in the classification of shaking/tremor severity.

Data Collection
Data collection is performed through serial communication, where Python receives the 10
bytes sent by the board, decodes them, and saves the data in an Excel file.
First, a serial connection is established, and then the ”Start” command is sent to the board,
after which data collection occurs for a predetermined, pre­chosen duration of time. To
that duration, 2 seconds are added, which will be cut off at the ends of the signal. This
is done after filtering the signal to remove edge artifacts created by the filter. This data
collection happens in a while loop, and in each iteration, the following occurs:
1. Read the 10 bytes that were sent using ser.read(10)
2. Decode the information that was read using struct.unpack('<Lhhh', data)
3. Convert the time to seconds and the accelerations to m/s2
When decoding the message, the code Lhhh is used, as L refers to an unsigned long
(time) and h to signed shorts (accelerations). The conversion of the timestamps is done
by dividing the time by 1,000,000 since it was sent in microseconds, and the conversion
of the accelerations was done by first multiplying by the sensitivity to convert into g and
then multiplying by the gravitational acceleration to convert to m/s2. The gravitational
acceleration is obtained from the SciPy.constants library and has a value of 9.80665
m/s2.

For the acceleration signals used only for visualization, the data processing consists of
removing the mean of the signals using np.nanmean(), which will remove the DC shift
created by gravity, and then applying a bandpass filter between 0.8 and 29 Hz. To do this,
a distinct filtering function was created to only filter the parts that are not NaN, otherwise
these will propagate thoughout the whole signal. To achieve this, a mask with the good
parts of the signal is created and only that is filtered. The filtered signal is then reapplied
to a previously created NaN copy of the original one. To finish, the first and last seconds
are cut from the filtered signal to remove edge artifacts created by the filter.

4. Remove the first and last seconds of recording

• Center frequency: the Center Frequency is calculated by finding the frequency that
separates the power of the signal in half. To do this, the function np.cumsum() is
applied to the PSD to find the cumulative power at each index. Then, the function
np.where() is used with the argument cumulative_power >= total_power/2 to
find the index of the frequency that is closest to the center frequency. Finally, the
desired frequency is selected from the frequencies array that was obtained when
calculating the PSD.
• Peak frequency: the peak frequency is the maximum frequency in the frequency
spectrum, and it is calculated by finding the maximum value of the PSD with the
function np.argmax(), and then selecting the associated frequency.
• Standard Deviation of Center Frequency: to calculate this index, it is necessary
to first find how much 66% of the total power is, and then the interval around it is
extended until the right frequency interval is found. This extension process is done
iteratively, where the interval increases symmetrically around the CF until 66% of
the total power is inside it. After, the standard deviation is calculated as half of the
interval around the center frequency.
• Harmonic Index: this is calculated using equation 2.1 for a normalized PSD.
• Sample Entropy (SampEn): Sample Entropy is calculated using the function SampEn
from the toolkit EntropyHub [71], which calculates it based on [72, 73]. This index
measures the randomness of the collected signal. A pathological tremor should
not be random, as it has a defined frequency and a spectrum similar to that of a
harmonic signal, unlike a physiological one.

Chapter 6: Discussion
In this section, a brief discussion of the results presented previously will be provided.
Regarding noise and shielding, it can be observed that, as indicated by the graphs and
TI calculations, shielding with aluminum foil does not decrease noise values. This could
be an indication that the measured noise is not electrical in origin. One could also think
that it can be removed using a filter. However, it can be observed that this noise is also
present in the frequencies of interest, indicating that it cannot be completely eliminated.
However, it is also in the millimeter order, which is smaller than human tremor, as can be
seen in Table 5.4.
When comparing the results from the new device’s sensors with the BK sensor and ex­
amining Table 5.1, it is evident that the error obtained using the acceleration table and the
standard shaker is not identical. In fact, the errors are much smaller when using the accel­
eration table. Although it is possible that the error increases with an increase in frequency,
this can also be attributed to the fact that the sensors are not as secure in the standard
shaker as they are in the acceleration table. Nevertheless, for both scenarios, the values
obtained from the sensors on this device are comparable to the ones being measured
using the calibrated BK sensor. This indicates that the device can accurately measure
acceleration and frequencies, which are essential aspects of tremor characterization.
When looking at Table 5.2, it’s also possible to observe that, even though the accelera­
tion table has suffered mechanical changes, and the fact that the CATSYS is a two­axis
system, the results are comparable. It is expected that the CATSYS measurements will
be larger than those obtained with the other sensors, due to the extra axis, but that is not
always the case. This can be attributed to the mechanical changes that the acceleration
table underwent. Nevertheless, it’s also possible to see that in the same position and
frequency, both the BK sensor and this device have results that are very close, further
indicating that the device being developed is able to measure acceleration accurately.
Now, an analysis of the results obtained from patients will be made. However, it is impor­
tant to note that when measuring Rest tremor with the CATSYS system, artifacts occur
at the start and end of the recordings for most patients, which affects frequency domain
parameters. An example of these can be found in Figure 6.1, in which the patient did not
create the artifacts, but they were measured by the system.
Figure 6.1: Artifacts obtained when measuring rest tremor using the CATSYS system
Starting with the discussion of the effect of passing a bandpass filter, it is evident from the
example presented that this is essential for removing artifacts created by slow movements.
Although this device is intended for use in a controlled environment, it is normal and
54

expected that when measuring Postural and Intentional tremors, the patient will slightly
move their arm, creating small drifts that need to be corrected. If that isn’t done, the
acceleration signals will present higher variation, and consequently Tremor Intensity will
also be bigger.
Regarding the mean errors of Tremor Intensity, it would be expected that adding axes
to the system would lead to the biggest error values, as it would increase the distance
between this device and the CATSYS system. However, this is not necessarily the case
for this experiment. When examining all the measurements from all patients, the trend is
that using two axes yields lower values of TI compared to those measured using CATSYS,
while using three axes yields higher values than the ones obtained with CATSYS. It is
also noticeable that the error increases when the signals are filtered, which contradicts
expectations, as the signals are also filtered in the CATSYS system using a Butterworth
filter. These errors can have various sources, such as different sensor sensitivities, if the
sensor moved during recording, or even slightly different placements of the sensor on
the hand. It is also not expected that both systems will yield exactly the same results,
especially since the CATSYS system has been in use for many years without calibration,
which can affect the measurement results.
Examining the results from the comparison of frequency parameters between the two
devices, it is possible to observe that, with the exception of Rest tremor, this device was
able to deliver reliable results within acceptable errors in all other cases. The increased
Absolute Error on Rest tremor measurements comes from the artifacts referred to before,
shown in Figure 6.1, which drag the peak of the PSD to the lower frequencies. This is
known to be incorrect, as measurements made from PD and ET patients indicate that this
value should be at least 4 Hz, which was not observed in most cases.
Focusing now on the mean values of the frequency parameters for healthy volunteers, the
center frequency values for postural and intention tremor are lower than expected. This
value would ideally be higher, above 8 Hz. In addition, as can be seen in Figure 6.2, in
Rest tremor, the power of the PSD is much lower than in Postural and Intention tremor.
At the same time, it’s also possible to see that in Rest tremor, there is activity throughout
all the frequencies in the PSD, which is not seen on the other two measurements.
It can also be seen that, in the time domain measurements of Rest tremor, the amplitudes
are much smaller, especially for the x and z axes, the ones perpendicular to the arm.
For Postural and Intention tremors, it’s possible to see lower­frequency waves with higher
amplitude. As such, it’s reasonable to conclude that in Postural and Intention tremor,
when the arm is not resting on any surface, the actual tremor’s PSD is masked by the
movements of the arms to keep the desired position in place. Ideally, such artifacts would
be removed, but their frequencies fall within the desired bandwidth, which means they
can’t be filtered out.
For PD and ET patients, it’s possible to see that the results of CF are within expecta­
tions, that is, they fall into the intervals of pathological tremor for each of these tremor
syndromes, 4­6 Hz for PD and 4­12 Hz for ET.
Regarding the Standard Deviation of Center Frequency, it’s possible to see that it is bigger
for healthy volunteers than for other patients, as it is expected since, contrary to healthy
tremor, pathological tremor is rhythmic and will have its PSD centered around a peak as
in Figure 6.3. The larger STDEV CF in healthy people indicates that the spectrum is not
composed of a defined peak, but of a broader spectrum of frequencies.

can be used to distinguish between PD and ET tremors. The second goal is answered
when calculating entropy using the Sample Entropy method with the parameters m = 2
and r = 0.1, which is when the difference of values between the two pathologies is bigger.
Therefore, this is the one that will be used in this device to distinguish between the two
pathologies.

Chapter 7: Conclusion and Future Work
As stated throughout this report, it is essential to not only find the right diagnosis for tremor
but also to monitor its progression. However, the current goal standard process for doing
so, clinical evaluation, is highly subjective, highlighting the need for an objective way to
measure and analyse tremor. Although there are various options in the market for it, they
are usually designed for one specific type of pathology, as well as most of the research
being done. The CATSYS system overcomes that limitation, however, it is becoming old
and in need of a newer version.
This thesis presented the design and validation of a device for the objective assessment
of hand tremor in a clinical setting, which can be used for any pathology. During its de­
velopment, certain research questions needed to be answered:
• How can a MEMS­based accelerometer system be designed to accurately measure
and analyse hand tremors for clinical and research applications?
• What are the optimal sensor specifications and signal processing techniques to iden­
tify and analyse hand tremor?
• Can entropy be used to distinguish between Parkinson’s Disease and Essential
Tremor patients? And if so, what are the ideal parameters that enable it?
To answer the first question, the developed device utilizes accelerometers integrated into
an Arduino board, along with a Graphical User Interface (GUI) developed with user in­
put, which allows for the collection and analysis of tremor signals in both the time and
frequency domains. This system was validated in both laboratory and clinical conditions,
where it was first compared against a Brüel & Kjær sensor and then against the CATSYS
to collect data from real patients. In the end, it showed great potential and the ability to
accurately collect and analyse data.
The answer to the second question is that the sensor should have a range of ± 2g, which is
sufficient to capture tremor, and gives the highest sensitivity. Data collection should have
a sampling frequency of at least double the desired maximum frequency according to the
Nyquist theorem, so the ideal value, in this case, should be at least 100 Hz. Regarding
signal processing techniques, they need to be suited to analyse the given signals but also
allow for the collection of information relevant for clinical use. In the literature, it has been
found that some researchers use techniques such as Empirical Mode Decomposition [27],
which separates the signal into various signals of different frequency bands. Although the
achieved result was good, and the Hilbert Spectrum of it could potentially be used for
tremor analysis, this method doesn’t allow for the collection of the time and frequency
domain features that have shown to be invaluable for tremor assessment. Therefore,
using the PSD for frequency analysis is the best option as it allows the calculation of all
the referred to indices.
Finally, to answer question three, as shown in Section 5.5 Sample Entropy can be used as
an indicator to distinguish between PD and ET. This is a very nice addition to the device
as these two pathologies have overlapping results in the frequency domain, which makes
it hard to distinguish between them, especially in the early stages.
To conclude, this project developed a device capable of accurately capturing and analysing
tremor signals, which can be used in a clinical setting to objectively analyse tremor. This
58

has several applications, besides those referred to of diagnosis and monitoring of tremor,
which are done in a clinical setting and to which the GUI was mainly developed to, it can
also be used in clinical research for medicine and treatment development, as it can be
used to measure their effects. However, there is still work to be done to fully complete
this project, which will now be presented.
7.1
Future work
In this section, future development and testing will be described. Although this device
already shows great promise, further development is still needed.
Firstly, there is a need to add the touch recording table and the reaction time handles
described in Section 2.3. These are used to perform further testing when in doubt be­
tween diagnoses. The touch recording table is used to perform a test in which the patient
taps the recording drum, a circular object with a microphone on it, following a series of
”beeps” while performing pronation and supination. This test assesses a person’s ability
to perform these movements quickly and maintain a steady pace in response to the ”beep”
sounds. If that is not the case, it can indicate difficulty in starting movements and slow­
ness in performing them, which are characteristic traits of PD. The same can be tested
using the reaction time handle, but in a different context. Although in the CATSYS sys­
tem, the presence of these tests involves the need for more sensors, these tests can be
performed using the already existing accelerometers in the new device. The first test can
be measured by identifying the points in the signal where the acceleration in the z axis
crosses zero and changes direction. The second test can be made by asking the patient
to tap their finger, or even hand, against the table instead of pressing a button and finding
peaks in the acceleration signal.
Also in development, something that needs to be improved is the possibility of using this
device in a wireless setting. Wires can be disturbing for some patients, as they restrict their
ability to perform movements freely and sometimes create a mess during the test due to
tangling. Therefore, a version in which the sensors can be connected via Bluetooth would
be ideal.
Further in this topic, it might be valuable to also explore the possibility of personalized
filtering. This means, the possibility of filtering the signal specifically for each patient to
obtain more accurate information. Like this, artifacts in the 1 to 25 Hz interval could maybe
be removed by filtering off frequencies that are not relevant for a specific condition. This
could also avoid the need to delete some artifacts from data recording, thereby avoiding
the loss of data points.
Finally, the GUI also needs to be worked on. The one developed during the realization
of this project works, but can still be improved, especially in error handling, to make them
easier to understand by the users. It will also be necessary to add more windows to ac­
commodate the previously mentioned tests (hand pronation and supination, and reaction
time), as well as additional data analysis scripts adapted for them.
Some tests that need to be performed include how much data can be removed from the
Excel files without altering the results too much, what is a threshold in power that can
separate healthy from unhealthy patients, and potentially the use in patients with different
pathologies from PD and ET.
The first test was somewhat made during this project, however, the results depend a lot
on the signal itself. In tremors that are more severe, an artifact may not have as much
impact as it would in smaller tremors, and the regularity of the signal will also affect how
Using MEMS Technology for Tremor Analysis
59
==PAGE:72==
much and which parts can be removed. However, the removal of some seconds of signal
for small artifacts seemed to have no major impact. Nevertheless, this is something that
should be further studied and possibly integrated into the GUI so that the user can select
the section to remove directly, rather than having to search through the Excel file.
The second test came from the fact that in healthy patients, especially for Postural and
Intention tremors, due to movements of the arm, the tremor can resemble the early stages
of pathological tremor. However, there is a major distinction, which is the intensity of the
power in the PSD. Therefore, it would be valuable to find a value that could separate
healthy from pathological tremor, and even use that in the PSD plots representation to
scale the vertical axis.
Finally, it would be important to validate this device for different pathologies. In theory, it is
not designed for a specific one, however, the provided parameters for tremor assessment
were only tested for two conditions, and other parameters may need to be developed to
assess other causes of tremor.

# this function finds the center frequency where the power is divided 50/50
def center_freq(self, psd, freqs):
# calculate the cumulative sum of the PSD
cumulative_power
= np.cumsum(psd)
total_power

center_freq = freqs[idx]

# this function will calculate the standard deviation of the center
#frequency, which is the band around the center frequency in which 66%
#of the total power is dissipated.
def stdev_cf(self, psd, freqs, center_frequency):
