Abstract
Macroscopic models of neuronal networks are widely used to study synchroniza-
tion and the average spiking activity in large ensembles of neurons, as found in
the brain. Such networks are often grouped into populations of neurons with
similar properties, i.e., their intrinsic tendency towards spiking or quiescence
and their excitatory or inhibitory coupling to other neurons. Vertices of these
networks are often one-dimensional oscillators, e.g., the Theta neuron. How-
ever, two populations of intrinsically quiescent Theta neurons with excitatory
intra- and inter-population coupling have, to our knowledge, not been studied.
In this work, we obtain such a network by choosing the parameters of a recently
introduced ODE system accordingly. This system can be reduced to a four-
dimensional ODE system via the powerful Ott-Antonsen ansatz. We study the
rich bifurcation structure with numerical continuation. We show that despite
the symmetry in the parameters, asymmetric stable states, e.g., limit cycles
with a distinct amount of oscillatory activity in both populations, occur. Our
results add to the knowledge of the given neural model, which has already been
analyzed in other regions of the many-dimensional parameter space.

Introduction
1.1
Biophysical context
Neurons, or nerve cells, play a crucial role in multicellular organisms. They
are responsible for signal transmission and processing, thus enabling complex
functions such as the control of limbs, cognition, or maintaining a constant heart
beat. In many organisms under study, the majority of neurons is found in the
central nervous system, including the brain.
The signals that neurons produce and transmit to each other are of elec-
trical nature, constituted by action potentials propagating along the axon of a
neuron. The axon is relatively long in the peripheral nervous system, while it
is often very short in the central nervous system. Connections between neu-
rons – without which most functions of neurons could not be fulﬁlled – are
realized by either synapses or gap junctions, or as they have been called, e.g.,
in [14], chemical synapses and electrical synapses. The latter are fast connec-
tions between neurons, with special proteins tying the membranes of neighbour-
ing neurons together, allowing for the exchange of ions or molecules [13]. In
this work however, we will only pay attention to the former type, the chemical
synapse (henceforth just ”synapse”). A neuron usually comprises a network of
dendrites (see Fig. 1.1), which in turn host contact points to other neurons, the
synapses [4]. In a synapse, the presynaptic neuron emits a neurotransmitter into
the space between neurons, the synaptic cleft. The neurotransmitter travels the
synaptic cleft and binds to an ion channel in the membrane of the postsynaptic
neuron, which in turn mediates the ﬂow of ions in or out of the postsynaptic
neuron, which corresponds to an electric current (see Fig. 1.2). Importantly,
this postsynaptic transmembrane current can be excitatory or inhibitory.
The postsynaptic neuron will, if the inputs received at its several synapses are
predominantly excitatory, ﬁre an action potential along its axon. If it receives
mostly inhibitory input, it will not ﬁre an action potential, but remain quies-
cent. The axon can again touch the dendrite of another neuron in the form of
a synapse, where it ”triggers a complex chain of biochemical processing steps
that lead to a release of neurotransmitter” [4]. We have thus closed the circle of

based on the detailed analysis carried out on the neurons of squids in 1952. Alan

and two serial connections of a voltage source and a linear and nonlinear resistor
(the ion gates). The resulting ODE system is nonlinear and four-dimensional
in its original form, while there exist simpler or more complicated variations of
the model.
1.2
Type I and type II ﬁring
Neuronal models undergo bifurcations that separate them, roughly speaking,
into a quiescent and a spiking regime. Regardless of the concrete model, we
here want to denote the bifurcation parameter as I and the bifurcation point
as I∗. On the quiescent side of the bifurcation, the dynamics of the neuron rest
at a stable equilibrium. On the ”spiking” side of the bifurcation, the neuron
ﬁres a train of action potentials. This repetitive spiking behaviour immediately

reminds us of a stable limit cycle with the property that a subset of the limit
cycle exhibits fast rotation, while the dynamics slow down on the rest of the
cycle (also known as a relaxation oscillation). Here, a quantity of interest is
the spiking frequency, i.e., the reciprocal of the time span between two action
potentials. If the frequency depends continuously on I, i.e., the neuron begins to
spike at arbitrarily low frequency as I crosses I∗, we speak of type I behaviour.
If however the neuron jumps straight to a non-zero, ﬁnite frequency as soon
as I∗is passed, the neuron is called a type II neuron (see Fig. 1.3).
Type
I behaviour can be achieved by what [4] calls a ”saddle-node-onto-limit-cycle
bifurcation”. On one side of the bifurcation, a saddle and a node exist. On the
other side there is a stable limit cycle which passes through the region where
the saddle and the node have coalesced. In this region, the dynamics are still
slow, hence the arbitrarily low frequencies at the onset of spiking.
Type II behaviour can either be achieved by a ”saddle-node-oﬀ-limit-cycle
bifurcation” or a subcritical Hopf bifurcation. In the saddle-node-oﬀ-limit-cycle
bifurcation, a stable limit cycle coexists with the saddle and the node, and they
coalesce in a point somewhat distant to the limit cycle. With the stable ﬁxed
point destroyed, the dynamics ”jump” to the limit cycle, hence the nonzero ini-
tial spike frequency. At a Hopf bifurcation, the oscillation frequency is nonzero
because it is proportional to the imaginary part of the complex conjugate pair
of eigenvalues that pass the imaginary axis. In a supercritical Hopf bifurcation,
the amplitude starts arbitrarily low, but in contrast, a spike has a large am-
plitude. However, [21] points out that once I has enough distance to I∗, the
limit cycle has grown in size and can indeed model a spiking behaviour. In a
subcritical Hopf bifurcation, we remind ourselves that as higher order terms in
the normal form are taken into account, there can be a stable limit cycle coex-
istent with the unstable one [4] (the two coalesce in a saddle-node bifurcation
of cycles, see Fig. 1.4). To this stable limit cycle the neuron jumps as I∗is
passed, exhibiting a type II behaviour with nonzero frequency and amplitude.
The Hodgkin-Huxley model can, depending on modiﬁcations and reductions,
undergo a saddle-node-onto-limit-cycle or saddle-node-oﬀ-limit-cycle bifurca-
tion, while the FitzHugh-Nagumo model (a Hodgkin-Huxley model reduced to
two dimensions) undergoes a subcritical Hopf bifurcation [4].

1.3.3
Bursting and beating
Its spiking rhythm, capable of bursting, is one of the peculiarities of the Theta
neuron. If I is taken to be a periodic function, say,

as, e.g., done in [2], and f is suﬃciently small, then we are in the initial setting in
the beginning of Sec. 1.3.1 where a slow oscillation drives a fast one. The system
then exhibits bursting, i.e., a periodic change in the spiking frequency, where
the Theta neuron spikes faster when I(t) is around its maximum, followed by
quiescence or at least slower spiking when the driving oscillation I(t) is around
its minimum. The terms ”periodic bursting” and ”parabolic bursting” are often
used as well. Moreover, we speak of ”beating” in the limit where the spikes
become ”uniformly distributed” [6], i.e., the spiking frequency is constant. We
show this behaviour in Fig. 1.6. In the top panel, the parameters are chosen such
that the Theta neuron’s spiking frequency stays almost constant, i.e., the neural
dynamics can approximately be described as beating. In contrast, the neural
dynamics in the lower panel exhibit episodes of noticeable relative quiescence,
i.e., we see an example of bursting.
Interestingly, I(t) need not be sinusoidal. More details about the eﬀect of
the waveform of the slow oscillation on the spiking pattern of the fast oscillation
can be found in [6].

dergone periodically, i.e., the stable and unstable equilibria periodically appear
on the limit cycle (making the neuron rest) and coalesce (making the neuron

1.3.4
Limitations
If we choose a too high frequency f, then an important assumption of the EK
reduction is violated, namely that the driving oscillation be slow compared to
the dynamics of the limit cycle of the neural oscillation. The result can be seen
in Fig. 1.7: The Theta neuron now oscillates more slowly than the ”slow” driving
oscillation. Therefore, choosing a too high frequency f of the driving oscillation
causes the Theta neuron to be an invalid representation of the high-dimensional
system it was supposed to reduce.
If biophysical realism is desired, another limitation of the Theta neuron is
that |I| may not be too high. This is because the EK reduction assumes a weak
perturbation away from the state in which a saddle-node exists on the limit
cycle.
1.4
Structure of this thesis
The basics of neural modelling with the aid of dynamical systems, as well as the
Theta neuron as an important one-dimensional model, have been introduced. In
this work, our focus is not on the electrochemical processes inside the individual
neuron, nor do we look at the details of the voltage signals that neurons send to
one another. Rather, we are interested in the macroscopic behaviour of neural
populations, i.e., we want to make statements about the average spiking activity,
and the amount of synchrony therein. Such macroscopic models have received
much attention in previous studies. Abstract macroscopic models of coupled
oscillators can provide a ”unifying explanation of oscillatory phenomena in the
human cortex” [9].
In the following chapter, we motivate our model equations. In Chapter 3

The system under study
2.1
Motivation
The Hodgkin-Huxley model, with its many dimensions, would be an inconve-
nient candidate for modelling large networks of neurons. We will choose the
Theta neuron as it is one-dimensional, yet conserves the mechanisms for type I
spiking.
An abstract macroscopic model sacriﬁces biophysical realism at the level of
the individual neuron as well as in the connections between the neurons, but
it can help to understand general phenomena that might occur across neural
architectures.
That raises the question with what coupling function and topology these
Theta neurons shall be connected in the network. Diﬀerent coupling topologies
have been studied in the literature, e.g., random graphs and all-to-all-coupled
graphs. The latter are not realistic at ﬁrst sight, since a network in which every
vertex is connected to every other vertex has N(N −1)/2 edges, which would
be an astronomic number in a brain of 1011 neurons. Even if the neural network
models only a region of the brain – as is usually the case – it would be unrealistic
to assume that every neuron keeps a synapse to every other neuron. However,
if each edge in an all-to-all-connected graph is associated with a value that
corresponds to the strength of the connection between the respective vertices,
the graph becomes more realistic because some connections can be modelled
stronger than others.
When studying neural dynamics at a meso- or macroscopic level, it is com-
mon to group neurons into clusters with similar properties. These clusters have
also been called ”communities” or ”populations”. We will stick with the lat-
ter term. Real-world counterparts of populations of neurons with homogeneous
properties can be found in the visual, somatosensory or motor cortex [4]. Im-
portantly, neurons of the same population are not necessarily spatially close to
each other.
A study carried out on newborn rats [18] suggests that neurons are organized
in networks where populations of densely connected neurons are interconnected

are more numerous than connections between neurons from distinct populations.
This can be modelled in an idealized fashion by an all-to-all coupled network

Another question is whether our neural network model should take the
spatial distance between neurons into account. The Theta neuron (1.2) does
not contain any spatial variables and can therefore be imagined as a zero-
dimensional point in space, i.e., in the brain.
Some studies, e.g., [12], have
however considered a spatially dependent coupling function between the Theta
neurons. We will not include any spatial dependence in the coupling, our model
will be agnostic to the spatial distance between neurons, it will only consider
whether two neurons are coupled strongly or weakly. Moreover, we will assume
the coupling topology to be constant. Even though learning and neural plastic-
ity play a role in the brain, one can choose a fast enough time scale so that no
learning, but other interesting dynamics (such as synchronization) occur. More-
over, not the entirety of neural circuitry is open to change through learning and
plasticity [18].
Another notion that comes with networks of neurons – or oscillators in gen-
eral – is synchrony. The term expresses the level of simultaneous activity in
the network. With the Theta neuron being a phase oscillator model, synchrony
in our system is easily deﬁned as the degree to which oscillators are in-phase,
i.e., a population synchronizes when the phases of its neurons draw closer to-
gether. The population is perfectly synchronous if all its neurons have the same
phase. Synchrony is important for various reasons. Firstly, a single neuron’s
signal is too weak to be measured non-invasively [16], neural activity will not
be detected by imaging techniques unless several neurons act at the same time.
Secondly, synchrony is involved in both functional and dysfunctional brain ac-
tivity [5], overly synchronous activity in the brain is believed to be correlated
to pathologies as Parkinson’s or epilepsy. An especially interesting case occurs

positively couple into the dynamics of other neurons, while neurons of inhibitory
populations seek to drive other neurons into the quiescent regime. In [3], one
driving population inﬂuences a driven population without feedback from the
driven into the driving population. In [27], an excitatory population is coupled
bidirectionally with an inhibitory one such that both populations oscillate, with
the inhibitory one trailing behind slightly. In [29] (Fig.4), two inhibitory pop-

coupling strength, and the reaction of the network to external periodic forcing
is studied. To our knowledge, the combination ”intrinsically quiescent Theta
neurons with excitatory intra- and inter-population coupling strength” has not
been studied before, but might still be relevant biologically, e.g., if two subsets
of the brain stimulate each other.
We therefore raise the following research
question.
What dynamics and what synchronization patterns can be observed in a net-
work that is an idealization of two populations of neurons with intrinsic tendency
towards quiescence, but with positive intra-population coupling and weakly pos-
itive inter-population coupling?

changes as parameters are varied. In doing so, bifurcations of codimensions 1
or 2 can be found. The software package that we use for numerical continuation
is MATCONT [26].

5.3
Bifurcation curves
The concepts introduced in Sec. 5.1 can be generalized to two simultaneously
active parameters. Assume that a degenerate equilibrium point in which a cer-
tain bifurcation happens is given. If a neighbouring point is to hold the same
degeneracy, two parameters will have to be manipulated simultaneously.
In
other words, there is a one-dimensional manifold in Rd+2 such that every point
on the manifold is an equilibrium in which a bifurcation of the given type oc-
curs. We call such a manifold a bifurcation curve. MATCONT can approximate
points on a bifurcation curve by continuing a degenerate equilibrium in two ac-
tive parameters. Along this curve, it can detect codimension-2-bifurcations. For
example, if a saddle-node bifurcation curve (a curve of saddle-node equilibria),
is continued, MATCONT can detect a cusp bifurcation.

Limit cycles
In [1], stable limit cycles of the Kuramoto order parameter Z have been called
Collective Periodic Wave (CPW). These states are interesting because not only
the individual neurons, but also their macroscopic synchrony oscillates. While
the neurons revolve around the unit circle, their phases periodically draw closer
together and then spread out more uniformly.3
In MATCONT, limit cycles can be continued from Hopf bifurcations, and it
turns out that the Hopf bifurcations on HB1 are supercritical and we get stable
limit cycles on the right side of HB1, i.e., K > KHB1. In this section, we study
the limit cycles, their creation and destruction.

6.8
Codimension-2 bifurcations
6.8.1
Cusp and Bogdanov-Takens bifurcations
The cusp bifurcation CP (see Fig. 6.3, left panel) occurs at the point where
SNa1 and SNa2 become tangent and stop. At any cusp bifurcation point, one
eigenvalue of the Jacobian is 0+0i, since saddle-node-curves meet in this point.
A condition on the normal form coeﬃcients of the saddle-node-bifurcation has
to be fulﬁlled as well [31], therefore it is a codimension-2 bifurcation. However,
in CP more than that happens: not only SNa1 and SNa2, but also HB2 meet
in this point. It can be checked in the eigenvalues along HB2 that not only one,
but three eigenvalues become 0 + 0i in CP. This necessarily means that µ, the
real part of the complex conjugate pair of eigenvalues on HB2, becomes zero in
CP. Since asymmetric states are only observed between SNa1 and SNa2, and
the Hopf curves also originate from CP, the point CP marks the upper edge of
the region of existence of asymmetric states.

Two Bogdanov-Takens bifurcations (BT1 and BT2, see Fig. 6.3) are encountered

become 0+0i [24]. In a Bogdanov-Takens point, a saddle-node bifurcation curve,
a Hopf bifurcation curve and a homoclinic bifurcation curve become tangent, the
latter two stop in the Bogdanov-Takens point [30]. The Hopf curve continues
as a neutral saddle curve6 (not shown in our ﬁgures). For the Bogdanov-Takens

Throughout this chapter, we have seen a rich bifurcation landscape. However, it
is unclear how much this landscape depends on the symmetric choice of param-

It would be an interesting direction for future work whether the curve SNoC

Conclusion
In this work we analyzed a macroscopic model of a network of two interact-
ing neural populations. Our aim was to study the bifurcation structure of the
overall ﬁring activity (or, equivalently, the synchrony) of the network. We were
especially interested in asymmetric states, where both populations behave dif-
ferently. While ”our” model is strongly inspired by the ones studied in [1, 2, 3],
it has – to the best of our knowledge – never been studied for the parameter
values chosen in this work. We investigated how we can correctly perform the
popular Ott-Antonsen reduction on the model and obtained the closed four-
dimensional ODE system (4.5), in accordance with [3].
We showed that all
equilibria of system (4.5) must lie on a one-dimensional manifold in the lower
half of the unit disc. On this manifold, there is a trade-oﬀbetween synchrony
and ﬁring rate.
Under careful consideration of the capabilities and limitations of the software
package MATCONT, we investigated the bifurcation structure of the system.
Even though both populations have the same parameters, the system exhibits
asymmetric states.
This includes asymmetric limit cycles, in which the ﬁr-
ing rate of one population oscillates between a relatively high minimum and
maximum, while the other population remains almost silent. The bifurcation
structure of system (4.5) is quite rich, with several global and codimension-2
bifurcations. Moreover, many of these bifurcations persist as the symmetry in
the parameters is broken. MATCONT is not a trivial tool to use. We therefore
double-checked the bifurcation points and curves, especially the complicated
points BIF1-3, with the aid of bifurcation-theoretical considerations. Note that
there is no guarantee that our numerical continuations have found every bifur-
cation in the parameter region we studied.
Future work should consider realizations of the model with N < ∞in or-
der to check whether, and if so, how strongly and quickly, two populations of
realistically many neurons are attracted to the stable states seen in this work.
Moreover, our work can easily be generalized to more than two populations.
It would be interesting to take our choices of parameters as a starting point for
introducing a third population, which is at ﬁrst only weakly coupled to the other
