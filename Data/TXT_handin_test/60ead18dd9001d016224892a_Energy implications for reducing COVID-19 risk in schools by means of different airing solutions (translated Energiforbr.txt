Abstract
The currently ongoing COVID-19 pandemic has raised the matter of the airborne transmis-
sion of a disease and the means by which it may be prevented or minimized. It has been
well documented that ventilation with outdoor air and air cleaning are effective means
of reducing the risk of transmission, although such measures may negatively impact the
energy demand of the current building stock. The purpose of this study is to examine
the energy efficiency of different airing solutions applied to keep the risk of infection be-
low a certain threshold. An elementary school building north of Copenhagen, Denmark,
was chosen as the case study. This analysis was performed by using the Wells-Riley
model of infection to find the required air supply rates to meet the infection risk target
and subsequently simulating the energy performance of the building with IDA ICE. The
airing solutions tested ranked in the following order in terms of energy efficiency: nat-
ural ventilation with portable air cleaners, exhaust ventilation with portable air cleaners,
mechanical ventilation, mechanical ventilation with filtration and, lastly, exhaust ventila-
tion. Other factors such as noise, ease and cost of installation and thermal comfort must
also be considered when choosing one such solution for a building. This study provides
a framework by which similar investigations can be carried out across various types of
buildings and climates, as well as for different pathogens.

Introduction
SARS-CoV-2 emerged during the later months of 2019, with the first case being reported in
the Hubei province of China, on the 17th of November [1]. The virus spread rapidly across
the world, leading to the World Health Organization declaring COVID-19 as a pandemic
on the 11th of March, 2020 [2].
As of the 30th of May 2021, there have been 169,597,415 cases of COVID-19 worldwide,
resulting in 3,530,582 deaths [3].
Respiratory viruses such as SARS-CoV-2 exhibit three primary routes of transmission:
(a) Contact transmission,
(b) Droplet transmission,
(c) Airborne transmission [4].
Airborne transmission refers to an infection caused by exposure to small, virus-containing,
airborne respiratory droplets, which can remain suspended in the air over long distances
and periods. Traditionally, according to the WHO, the cut-off of to distinguish airborne
from droplet transmission is set to 5 μm, with airborne transmission referring to droplets
smaller than 5 μm [5]. However, this definition has been argued in connection with the
current pandemic, with researchers pointing out that it is perhaps more accurate to say
that the term ”droplet” should refer to particles that fall to the ground and the term ”aerosol”
should refer to particles that remain suspended in the air. This distinction means that the
cut-off point between the two would be 100 μm, with droplets being larger than 100 μm
and aerosols being smaller than 100 μm [6]. This also refocuses the conversation around
short-range (<1m) and long-range (>2m) transmission [7].
Regardless, airborne transmission is of particular concern with regard to closed indoor
spaces, such as office buildings and schools, where people assemble over a long period
of time[4], [8].
When referring to spaces such as the ones mentioned above, ventilation with outdoor
air is thought to be an effective measure to reduce airborne transmission of diseases,
with experts highlighting its importance in combating the COVID-19 pandemic [8], [9]. In
addition to supplying outdoor air to a space in order to dilute (reduce) the concentration of
aerosols laden with the virus, it is also possible to to filter these aerosols and effectively
remove the virus, for example by using a portable air cleaner, or by providing recirculation
and filtration of the indoor air through a mechanical ventilation system. As previously
imposed ”lockdowns”, intended to reduce the spread of COVID-19 worldwide, begin to
be lifted, it becomes clear that the application of mitigative strategies such as these can
make the gradual reopening of society easier and safer.
Schools, especially elementary schools which are the focus of this study, and kinder-
gartens, are some of the first institutions to return to operation worldwide. Among the
reasons cited for this are the lower prevalence of symptomatic COVID-19 among children
[10] and the negative impact of school closure on the mental health and development of
children [11].
Increased or otherwise modified ventilation (e.g. filtering), along with other measures that
can be taken to curtail the risk of COVID-19 infection in a school setting, may result in a

higher energy consumption than would be observed under other circumstances.
As of 2018, the energy consumption of buildings amounts to 36% of the worldwide final en-
ergy use [12]. The application of increased outdoor ventilation rates to curb the pandemic
to a large part of the existing building stock could both exacerbate the current energy crisis
and may lead to unfavorable indoor environment conditions, which are not acceptable ac-
cording to building codes worldwide. Other methods of providing air cleaning may also
have considerable energy or indoor environment implications. Thus, the investigation of
various types of measures to combat the COVID-19 pandemic is important to document
their impact on the energy use of buildings.

1.1.2
Wells-Riley model of infection
The risk of COVID-19 infection through aerosol transmission can be calculated using a
version of the Wells-Riley model of infection, which correlates the probability of infection
(P) with the total quantity of virus inhalded, expressed as quanta (ntot) [13]:
P = 1−e−ntot .
(1.1)
Wells originally developed the model while studying tuberculosis, using a Poisson distri-
bution to define the notion of a quantum. Each quantum represents a number of infective
particles inhaled that result in a 63.2% chance of infection [22]. The typical formation of
the equation relies upon the assumption of a steady-state of quanta concentration in the
space and does not take into account virus removal mechanisms other than ventilation
with outdoor air, but this will be further elaborated in section 2.3.

Quanta emission rates
The estimation of quanta emission rates for SARS-CoV-2 is a subject which many re-
searchers have looked into, with varying results. This parameter presents a challenge,
as it can only be back-calculated in light of previous known outbreaks. However, as men-
tioned, not all environment parameters and virus removal mechanisms are known in every
case that is studied, nor is it always possible to account for other transmission routes such
as droplet or contact transmission.
In an oft-cited study, Miller et al. (2021) [13] investigated a superspreading event taking
place in Skagit Valley, Washington, USA, with 33 confirmed and 20 possible secondary
infections arising from a single infected person that had attended a choir meeting. By use
of the Wells-Riley model and a Monte Carlo simulation, the mean quanta emission rate
for this event was calculated at 970 quanta/hour, a high value consistent with this being
a superspreading event.
Conversely, Buonanno et al. reported much lower values in two studies [15], [20]. The
first study presented quanta emission rates as a function of the viral load in sputum, con-
cluding that relatively high (>100 quanta/h) rates could be found in subjects performing
light exercise and speaking, whereas lower emission rates (1 quantum/h) were likely to be
observed in subjects at rest. The second study presented more estimates, with median
values ranging from 0.37 quanta/h for a person breathing, at rest, to 32 quanta/h for a
person speaking loudly while performing a light activity.
Miksewski (2021) [23], presented a range of predicted quanta emission rates, following a
longnormal distribution with median values from 0.21 quanta/h, to 17 quanta/h, depend-
ing on the respiratory activity. The values inferred from 14 known outbreaks were then
examined, to determine at which percentile of the lognormal distribution of the predicted
quanta emission rates they correspond. These quanta emission rates were also com-
pared to the corresponding rates for other diseases, with the study finding that the median
quanta emission rate of SARS-CoV-2 is lower than those of untreated tuberculosis, aden-
ovirus and measles, but higher than SARS-CoV-1, MERS, treated tuberculosis, influenza,
coxsackievirus and rhinovirus.
Dai and Zhao (2020), on the other hand, estimate values in the rage of 14-48 quanta/h
for a person in a sedentary state [24], higher than those reported by other studies for a
similar activity.
In a study examining the robustness of the six-foot social distancing rule, imposed by
the CDC, against airborne transmission, Bazant and Bush (2021) [25], provided a range
of estimates, according to activity, for the quanta concentrations found in a subject’s ex-
haled breath, deduced from drop-size distributions reported in other studies. These val-
ues range from 1.1 quanta/m3, for nose breathing to 142 quanta/m3 for loud speech, while
the study also makes reference to the Skagit valley choir super-spreading event, with a
quanta concentration of 970 quanta/m3. These findings are not immediately comparable
to the others referenced in this section, as they refer to quanta concentrations in exhaled
breath and not emission rates and the subjects’ breathing rate needs to be taken into
account. Using the breathing rates suggested by the authors, this range corresponds to
0.55 quanta/h for nose breathing to 106.5 quanta/h for loud speech, which is consistent
with the findings of Buonnano et al. [15].
Overall, there is significant variation in the values presented by the studies above and
Miller et al. (2020) [13], as well as Miksewsky et al. (2021) [23] suggest the possibility
of emission rates at very high percentiles during known outbreaks. Table 1.2 presents a

1.1.3
Risk assessment and ventilation
The estimation of the emission rate of quanta for SARS-CoV-2 serves the purpose of
assessing the risk of infection associated with various activities. While assessing risk
on a population level involves factors such as the probability of an infected person being
present in a space and the overall susceptibility of a population, several researchers have
attempted to quantify the risk of infection on a smaller scale, for a variety of scenarios.
A recommendation established by Federspiel (2020) [9] aims to establish a criterion by
which the number of secondary infections from a single infected person is below 1, for
the entirety of the infected person’s infectious period and all of their activities. This cri-
terion takes takes into account removal mechanisms and activities generating the risk and
utilizes the Wells-Riley model of infection and the SIR model of epidemic dynamics.
In the same study as cited earlier, Bazant and Bush (2021) quantified the role of mask
wearing and increased ventilation rates to curtail transmission in two scenarios: a typical
American classroom and a nursing home. With all other parameters kept the same, in-
creasing the ventilation rate from 0.34 air changes per hour (natural ventilation) to 8 air
changes per hour (mechanical ventilation) increased the duration of time that one can

safely spend in a classroom from 1.2 hours to 7.2 hours [25]. In combination with masks,
the authors suggest that this would render transmissions within the school rare.
Dai and Zhao (2020) also calculated the ventilation rates required to keep the risk of
infection in a space below 1%, for a given duration of time, with quanta emission rates in
the range of 14 to 48 quanta/h. For a single infectious person an exposure time of 0.25h,
the required ventilation rate was found to be 100-350 m3/h, while for an exposure time
of 3h, the required ventilation rate was 1200-4000 m3/h [24]. In almost all scenarios, it
was shown that it is possible to achieve a 1% infection probability over the space’s typical
occupancy time via ventilation.
In a project conducted by the California Department of Public Health in 2020 [26], different
ventilation scenarios were considered with reference to a hypothetical classroom. These
scenarios included a range of ventilation rates from 2.8 l/s-person to 10.5 l/s-person,
MERV 8 or MERV 13 filters and portable air cleaners and mask wearing. An additional
”no ventilation” (0.2 air changes per hour / ACH) scenario for classrooms that do not have
a mechanical ventilation system installed was considered. Due to the uncertainty associ-
ated with quanta emission rates, the study focused on relative risk. The results, presented
in Figure 1.1, strongly highlight the importance of ventilation, but also, more importantly
that of filtration and mask usage in lowering the risk of transmission.

In a similar line of investigation, Miksewski et al. (2021) [23] calculated the reproduction
number pertaining to two different scenarios, one in a classroom and one in an army
barracks, under different ventilation rates. This analysis showed that increased ventila-
tion rates are capable of drastically reducing the number of secondary infections in both
scenarios, although in both cases ventilation alone was insufficient to bring the reproduc-
tion number below 1 if the infected person was assumed to be standing and speaking as
opposed to breathing at rest.
While the studies above have attempted to approach the matter of infection risk over
a given duration of time in occupying a space, they do not account for changes in oc-
cupancy within that duration. Melikov et al. (2020) [27] broached this topic, suggesting
that increased class duration in a classroom is detrimental to risk management, while in-
creased break duration is beneficial. The study also concurs that higher ventilation rates,
as well as lowered occupancy, are associated with a lower risk of infection.

1.1.4
Energy consumption and indoor environment quality
Changes in the operation of a building, such as the ones described above, can affect
the energy consumption of the building, particularly since risk management may require
unusually high ventilation rates.
Balocco and Leoncini (2020) [28] studied a historic, naturally ventilated school building
in Italy, proposing different control strategies for a mechanical ventilation system, includ-
ing different temperature setpoints and setbacks and modulation of the air change rate
between 2 and 4 ACH. The results show that, while a system with a continuous air tem-
perature and ventilation control mode could lead to an increase of 57 kWh/m2 during the
heating season, the optimal combination of intermittent air temperature control and atten-
uated ventilation only led to an increase of 5 kWh/m2. These results are significant, as
they underscore the need for conscious decision-making when retrofitting a building to
respond to a situation such as the current COVID-19 pandemic.
A study by Mokhtari and Jahangir (2021) [29] analyzed different building occupancy sched-
ules and air exchange rates provided by the HVAC system in conjunction with the infection
risk in a university building. A uniform occupancy distribution pattern was shown to reduce
the number of infected people by up to 56% and the energy consumption by up to 32%.
With regard to the air change rate specifically, an air change rate of 10 h−1 was simulated
to result in an added HVAC energy consumption of approximately 6.1 kWh/m2 within 5
days compared to no ventilation, with a decrease of approximately 400 infected people.
Aviv et al. (2021) [30] suggest a shift in the ventilation paradigm from centralized mech-
anical ventilation systems to natural ventilation assisted by radiant cooling, as a response
to the COVID-19 pandemic. With a focus on hot and humid climates, the study highlights
the high cooling energy costs of increasing the outdoor air supply in conventional HVAC
systems. On the other hand, the natural ventilation and radiant cooling systems sugges-
ted by the authors, when applied to 60 major cities worldwide, were projected to lead in a
decrease in energy costs by 10–45% compared to a mechanical, central AC system.
The subject of indoor environment quality goes hand-in-hand with that of energy con-
sumption. Alonso et al. (2021) [11] used two classrooms in Spain to perform a case study
of air quality and thermal comfort during, comparing these conditions before the pandemic
and during it. The necessary addition of natural ventilation to adhere to the recommen-
ded CO2 values during the pandemic led to a significant increase in the hours below
the thermal comfort limit, particularly when the HVAC system was turned off. No energy
simulations were performed in this case.
Also tackling the subject of ventilation and indoor environment quality in classrooms, Gao
et al. [31] studied four different methods of supplying ventilation (manually operable win-
dows, mechanical ventilation, automatically operable windows and automatically operable
windows with an exhaust fan), in order to determine which results in the best perception
of the indoor environment by the students in a Danish climate. The system that performed
the best was found to be the automatically operable windows with an exhaust fan.
In a similar vein, ASHRAE Research Project 1624-RP (2017) [32] was an intervention

The existing literature illustrates the importance of ventilation systems, control schemes

impact on the energy consumption of a building. While studies that focus on actionable
results for decision-makers seeking energy efficient methods of pandemic risk manage-
ment when assessing HVAC systems for existing buildings can be found, there is no real
uniform methodology of assessment of their energy efficiency.
1.2
Objective and scope of work
The primary objective of this study was to evaluate the energy cost of different methods
of providing clean air to an elementary school classroom with the purpose of reducing
COVID-19 risk. A classroom was chosen as the case study here, specifically due to the
tendency and need for schools to remain open, even under pandemic conditions.
In order to make comparisons between different airing solutions possible, it was deemed
suitable to set a target to be achieved through each solution. A disease’s basic reproduc-
tion number, R0, refers to the number of secondary infections arising in a population from
a single infected person, with no population immunity or interventions [26]. In general
terms, if this number is above 1, then the total number of infections in a population will
rise, while if it is below 1, the total number of infections will fall.
For the purposes of this study, the goal for each airing solution was set as such: For any
one infected student entering a classroom over the course of their infectious period, the
number of secondary infections arising should be equal to or lower than 0.9, assuming
some risk of infection would occur outside of the classroom. This reproduction number
will henceforth be referred to as the community risk, or classroom reproduction number,
Rc.
Only airborne transmission was taken into account in this case. While the present case
used COVID-19 as the model disease upon which assumptions were based, the same
framework can be applied to other infectious, airborne diseases. This will be discussed
further in latter sections.

avoiding potentially low temperatures during class.

2.4.1
Air supply rate limitations
The air supply rate is given in ACH, or air changes per hour. The level of air supply (λv)
is set in order to achieve the objective outlined in section 1.2. For scenario 1, since the
natural ventilation rate achieved via window opening cannot be directly controlled, it is
instead found through simulation.
An additional constraint is that, according to the Danish Building Regulation of 2018
(BR18), the outdoor air flow (λvo) must be equal to or higher than 5.0 l/s per person plus
0.35 l/s per m2 of the space. In this case, this requirement means that the minimum
outdoor air ventilation rate for each classroom is equivalent to 2.37 ACH or 1.97 l/s/m2.
This requirement is calculated using the lowered occupancy status of the school, con-
sidering that the building does not have an existing ventilation system installed and the
purpose of its installation is only to prevent the rapid spread of infection during a pan-
demic. It should be noted that this building, being an older building, currently does not
meet the code requirements. Therefore, the above requirement does not need to be met
in the case of natural ventilation (scenario 1), but must be met for the cases of exhaust
and mechanical ventilation (scenarios 2 and 3).
As an upper bound to the air supply rate, it was assumed that it is unrealistic to provide
more than 15 ACH, considering that higher air flows might cause heavy draughts and
temperature and noise issues.

4
Discussion
Several airing (ventilation, air cleaning and filtration) solutions were investigated with re-
gard to their energy performance under the conditions of the COVID-19 pandemic, with
the objective of securing a low risk of infection as set forth in section 1.2.
4.1
Evaluation of airing solutions
In terms of energy performance, examining the results presented in section 3.3, under the
assumptions of this study, points to a relatively clear image regarding the ranking of each
scenario:
1. Scenario 1b (Natural ventilation with air cleaners), which performs the best across
all air supply rates.
2. Scenario 2b (Exhaust ventilation with air cleaners), which is 2nd best for 2 out of 3
air supply rates.
3. Scenarios 3a (Mechanical ventilation) and 3b (Mechanical ventilation with recircula-
tion and filtering), which only show minor differences in performance between them.
4. Scenario 2a (Exhaust ventilation, which performs the worst across all air supply
rates.
The results of further analysis, assessing the cumulative energy cost of reducing the risk
of infection to a certain threshold, in section 3.4 point to the same ranking, but place
scenario 3a marginally ahead of scenario 3b.
As regards the individual performance of each scenario, in more detail:
• Scenario 1b represents the most minimal change that can be made to a building that
relies on natural ventilation. The only increases in energy consumption correspond
to equipment loads, which are minor. There are some, also minor, decreases in
heating energy consumption, presumably due to the heat generated by the portable
air cleaner units’ operation.
• Scenario 2a performs poorly, particularly with regard to heating energy. The air en-
tering the classrooms due to the pressure difference created by the exhaust vent-
ilation is untreated and, given Copenhagen’s climate, quite cold. As such, heating
energy demand rises significantly.
• Scenario 2b, being in essence a combination of 1b and 2a, only exhibits a small
increase in heating, equipment and HVAC auxiliary energy. At higher air supply
rates, this scenario outperforms both mechanical ventilation scenarios, due to the
high demand in HVAC auxiliary energy required for mechanical ventilation.
• Scenario 3a exhibits a small increase in heating energy compared to the reference
scenario, but its HVAC auxiliary demand represents the highest contribution in its
overall energy consumption.
• Scenario 3b performs very similarly to scenario 3a, with two minor differences. The
heating energy for 3b is slightly lower than that for 3a, owing presumably to the
smaller energy demand to heat the air supplied to the classrooms, as a large part
of it is recirculated and therefore already treated. Still, this difference is small, as
Energy implications for reducing COVID-19 risk in schools by means of different airing solutions
31
==PAGE:44==
scenario 3a already has a high efficiency heat recovery system (90%). On the other
hand, the HVAC auxiliary energy here is slightly higher than in scenario 3a, due to
the increased pressure difference caused by the presence of the filter.
In addition to the above, several other matters should be taken into consideration when
assessing different airing solutions to be applied. When referring to a classroom, as in
this study, noise levels are an important parameter to be taken into account. As such,
portable air cleaners can be disruptive to the operation of the class. For example, the
Medify MA-112 Air Purifier, used in both levels of the sensitivity study due to its high
Clean Air Delivery Rate, has a maximum noise level of 70 dB [47]. This is a significant
drawback of scenarios 1b and 2b, which renders them far less suitable for a classroom
environment. On the other hand Toftum and Wargocki (2017) [32], examining the same
classrooms, did not find that the noise levels differed greatly in the classrooms when the
mechanical ventilation and exhaust ventilation were activated, compared to when they
were idled. These findings suggest that scenarios 2a, 3a and 3b would likely not exhibit
issues related to noise.
The thermal comfort of the occupants is also a significant factor influencing the choice
of ventilation method. This is especially pertinent in the case of scenario 2a, which in-
volves high supply rates of untreated air. In addition to increasing the energy consump-
tion required for heating, this causes thermal discomfort, as seen in section 3.3, and likely
draught issues, especially at higher air supply rates. These issues are less likely to occur
when using a mechanical ventilation system, where the setpoint for supplied air can be
defined. Indeed, the results show a small improvement of thermal comfort with the use of
mechanical ventilation systems.
While it is beyond the scope of this study to assess the financial cost and benefits of
each ventilation system, it should be kept in mind that an increase in the building’s energy
demand will result in an increased cost. At the same time, however, the negative effects of
low ventilation rates, which are usually observed in a building relying on natural ventilation
such as this, on the learning performance of students and overall health and productivity
of building occupants have been well documented [50]–[52].
In a study pertaining to office buildings in the USA, McArthur (2020) [53] found an eco-
nomic net benefit related to productivity increase brought on by an increase in ventilation
rates across a wide range of climates, although the economic benefit of an increase in
the learning performance of elementary school students is harder to quantify than in the
case of a working adult. A higher risk of infection will result in higher rates of absenteeism
in the school. At the same time, airing solutions with provide higher outdoor air supply
rates (such as 2a and 3a) will have increased benefits to the learning performance of the
pupils.
Regarding the ease of implementation of each solution, scenario 1b is most likely the
simplest to implement and possibly the cheapest. The scenarios involving an extract
fan (2a and 2b) are also relatively simple to implement, while the scenarios relying on
a mechanical ventilation system require the most extensive work. Installation costs are
likely to follow the same pattern, though this is not investigated here.
4.1.1
Summary of evaluation
All scenarios assessed have a higher primary energy consumption than the reference
scenario (1a). This is in line with expectations, as increasing the air supply rate should
lead to an increase in energy consumption. In the case of the school building studied
here, under the specific conditions, the systems examined have clear advantages and
32
Energy implications for reducing COVID-19 risk in schools by means of different airing solutions
==PAGE:45==
drawbacks. The most energy efficient scenario simulated is the simple addition of portable
air cleaner units to each classroom. However, its implementation in a school building may
prove problematic, if it is not otherwise possible to reduce the noise levels produced by
these units, or find a model which produces lower noise levels. The next-best systems,
being those involving mechanical ventilation, are more difficult to implement in an existing
building and lead to a larger increase in energy consumption. Nevertheless, if the goal is
to provide a long-term ventilation system, these should also be considered. The scenario
relying on the use of an exhaust fan presents too high of an energy consumption and too
many issues regarding draughts and low temperatures to be viable without some other
supporting system such as e.g. heat recovery units (not implemented in this study). Lastly,
the use of an extract fan in combination with an air cleaner fails to provide any significant
benefit over the use of an air cleaner alone.
Since this study is meant to serve as a blueprint by which other, similar, investigations
may be carried out in other climates, occupation conditions, building types, etc., it should
be noted that the observations made above are not universal. For example, the noise
levels associated with the use of an air cleaner may not be problematic when referring
to other building types, such as a sports center or a gym. Similarly, in a warmer climate,
the implementation of a system involving an extract fan may be far less detrimental than
in the case of Copenhagen. On the other hand, if the building being examined already
has a ventilation system installed, then the question becomes how best to upgrade said
system (e.g. is it more efficient to upgrade the outdoor air delivery rate of a mechanical
ventilation system, or to install a filter and provide recirculated air?).
4.2
Risk assessment
The risk calculations presented so far only pertain to the risk of infection associated with
the case where an infected person is present in the classroom over the course of a week.
In order to assess the risk of infection at the level of the population, one must refer to the
number of active cases in a country and the fraction of the population that is susceptible
to infection. In the case of Denmark, Federspiel’s Healthy Buildings Guideline Calculator
(2020) [54], gives this number as 9832 as of the 5th of May 2021 and the percentage of
the population that is susceptible as 84%. The population of Denmark as of Q3 2020 is
5,825,337 [55], which means the percentage of the population currently infected is 0.17%.
By the application of Equation 1.1, with the same quanta emission rates and correspond-
ing air supply rates calculated earlier and the assumption that 84% of the occupants are
susceptible and 0.17% are infective, the absolute individual probability of infection for a
single day is 0.03%. Over the course of a week, the absolute probability of infection is
0.13%, corresponding to 0.02 COVID-19 cases arising. Over the course of all 200 school
days, it is 5.12%, corresponding to 0.73 COVID-19 cases arising.
In the case of Denmark, then, these values are currently very low. That said, there are
many countries in which the current pandemic situation is still dire and that this model can
serve as a tool for pathogens other than SARS-CoV-2.
4.3
Model extensions
The methodology of this study can be applied to any building across the world, regardless
of building type or occupancy schedule. A program such as IDA ICE can simulate different
climate conditions for cities worldwide. Provisions would need to be made to ensure
that the air supply rates and ventilation with outdoor air rates conform to the particular
regulations of the country at hand.
Energy implications for reducing COVID-19 risk in schools by means of different airing solutions
33
==PAGE:46==
Similarly, this model is applicable not only for COVID-19, but for other airborne diseases.
The changes that would need to be made would pertain to the quanta emission rates
corresponding to each pathogen, as well as its decay rate (k). The effect of deposition
to surfaces (λdep) would remain the same, provided the studied particle size range is the
same as in Chatoutsidou and Lazaridis (2019) [40]. Other strains of SARS-CoV-2 with
potentially different quanta emission rates can also be examined in the same manner.
Further investigations can be made in the form of sensitivity analyses regarding paramet-
ers not investigated in the sensitivity analysis performed here. For example, it is possible
to modify the ratio of clean air provided when examining solutions involving filtering. In
addition, factors such as mixing of children between classrooms, increased occupancy or
a different class schedule can be incorporated in future studies.
Alternative solutions to reduce infection risk which were not included in this study, such
as ultraviolet germicidal irradiation (UVGI), can be easily implemented in the first order
loss rate used to calculate the risk of infection.
4.4
Limitations
This project presents certain limitations that must be noted:
• IDA ICE, the program used to simulate the energy performance of the building, is not
able to simulate airflow patterns within each zone/room. This means that the above
analysis is carried out with the assumption of well mixed air in each classroom.
• This study pertains to a single climate zone.
• The models/types of air cleaners, fans and filters used in this study are only repres-
entative. Other models or types may give different results.
• The only route of transmission taken into account is airborne transmission and not
droplet or contact transmission. The behavior of the buildings’ occupants is a para-
meter which may heavily influence the results, especially considering the age of the
students and that they may be less reliable in following guidelines than adults.
• The length of the active infectious period for each person may vary, depending on the
severity of their symptoms, how early they quarantine, whether or not widespread
testing and contact tracing is deployed in the subject country, etc..
• Technical issues which may lead to improper functioning of the mechanical ventila-
tion system, e.g. imperfect fitting of the filter, incorrect operation or lack of mainten-
ance, were not taken into account.
• Information about the infectivity (quanta emission rates) of all strains of SARS-CoV-
2 is still incomplete and subject to correction by newer studies. The effectiveness
of vaccinations and their impact on this field of study is not assessed here.
• Quanta emission rates throughout the day were considered constant.
• The amount of quanta exhaled per unit of time or per volume of exhaled air is the
most uncertain parameter of this study, due to the limited time during which this
pathogen has been researched. Several papers have suggested different levels of
quanta exhaled according to the activity being performed, as detailed in subsec-
tion 1.1.2. Even by conducting a sensitivity analysis, considering realistic air supply
rates, it was only possible to investigate a small range of quanta emission rates in
this study, due to the restrictions placed on air supply rates.

Conclusions
Providing adequate ventilation and air cleaning to a building with no ventilation system
installed in order to prevent major disease outbreaks during a pandemic is a challenge,
especially regarding buildings that remain operational and house a large number of oc-
cupants. If the existing building stock is to be prepared to cope with the requirements of
safe operation in such times, an investigation into the most efficient ventilation methods
by which to meet said requirements is vital.
The aim of this research was to assess an array of ventilation systems (with outdoor air,
with or without filtering) as to their energy efficiency in achieving a number of secondary
infections arising equal to or lower than 0.9 from one infected student entering a classroom
over the course of a week.
The best system in terms of energy performance for this case study was found to be the
addition of air cleaners to classrooms ventilated via window opening. The worst system
in terms of energy performance was found to be the use of extract ventilation via an ex-
haust fan. In terms of energy cost compared to the reference case, lowering the risk of
infection to the target resulted in an increase of 1.0% to 7.8% at the lowest quanta emis-
sion rate assumption and 3.0% to 53.5% at the highest quanta emission rate assumption,
depending on the solution chosen. These findings show that a wide range of outcomes
is possible regarding the increase in energy consumption, depending on the ventilation
system applied. Still, the least costly in terms of energy scenarios did not present a large
increase in energy consumption.
Further investigation into this topic can include the modification of the operation schedule
of the building, in order to optimize occupancy with regard to infection risk. In addition, the
technical and financial issues arising from the implementation of an air treatment solution
will need to be addressed before any decisions are made for any individual building. One
thing that is clear, however, is that, through a thorough examination of infection risk and
energy cost, it is possible to avoid airing solutions that would be largely problematic and
costly in energy in the studied case, and instead opt for ones that are more efficient.
