Abstract
Background: Despite Osteoarthritis (OA) being a comprehensive worldwide burden, there is no
disease modifying treatment available for patients suﬀering from OA. Degradation of articular
cartilage is one of the major hallmarks of OA, and its preservation and restoration are paramount
to joint health. Throughout life, the articular cartilage is routinely subjected to mechanical forces
and to cell-regulatory growth factors. IGF-1 has shown to stimulate chondrocytes to synthesise
type II collagen, which oﬀers great potential for cartilage repair. Additionally, chondrocytes are
inﬂuenced by dynamic compression, but the eﬀect of compression on cartilage metabolism is not
fully understood. A previous study has suggested that the combination of IGF-1 and dynamic
compression may stimulate an additive eﬀect on formation of cartilage. Hereof, this study aims
to investigate the potential additive eﬀect of IGF-1 and dynamic compression on the cartilage
formation in a translational ex vivo bovine cartilage model.
Methods: Healthy bovine cartilage explants were cultured and treated with IGF-1 and intermittent
dynamic compression for 21 days. Supernatants were harvested on every second or third day and
stored for biomarker measurement. The cellular metabolic activity was measured in the supernatant
once weekly by AlamarBlue assay. The cartilage turnover was proﬁled by matrix derived neo-
epitope biomarkers which were measured directly in the supernatant. The glycosaminoglycan
(GAG) degradation was quantiﬁed in the supernatant by use of the dimethylmethylene blue
(DMMB) assay. Histological staining with Safranin-O/Fast Green was conducted in order to
support the experimental ﬁndings. Moreover, the ex vivo culture medium was improved with
anti-fungal agent to avoid fungal contamination.
Results: The cellular metabolic activity was stable for 21 days of culturing.
After several
optimisation steps of the IGF-1 concentrations and mechanical compression settings, IGF-1
and dynamic compression showed to stimulate the formation of type II collagen, separately.
Furthermore, when IGF-1 and dynamic compression were combined, an additive eﬀect on the
type II collagen formation was observed on day 21. IGF-1 and dynamic compression did not
appear to increase the GAG degradation. The histological staining revealed that explants were not
injured by dynamic compression. Chondrocytes treated with the maximum IGF-1 concentration
showed enlarged cell size in the deep cartilage zone, which may be an indication of hypertrophic
morphology. The addition of Fungizone (Amphotericin B) in the ex vivo culture medium showed
not to aﬀect the metabolic activity and the type II collagen remodelling. Moreover, Fungizone did
not interfere with the neo-epitope biomarker assay.
Conclusion: This study showed that stimulation with IGF-1 and dynamic compression in an
translational ex vivo bovine cartilage model induced an additive formation of type II collagen.
This suggests that the combination of biochemical and biomechanical stimuli may enhance the
formation of cartilage more eﬀectively. Thus, future OA drug development for either maintenance
or repair of articular cartilage should consider both biochemical and biomechanical stimulation
when assessing treatment eﬀect.

Introduction
1.1
Osteoarthritis
Osteoarthritis (OA) is a degenerative joint disorder advancing to chronic disability. OA is the
most prevalent arthritic disease worldwide and according to the Danish Arthritis Foundation
(Gigtforeningen) [1], more than 300.000 Danes have been diagnosed with OA. Furthermore, the US
Arthritis Foundation emphasises that more than 30 million Americans are aﬀected by OA [2]. The
World Health Organization (WHO) reports that symptomatic knee OA occurs in 9.6% of men and
18.0% of women aged over 60 years worldwide [3]. As the prevalence of OA increases with age and
the elderly population is growing, especially in high-income countries, the percentage of adults
with OA is expected to rise considerably [4]. OA is a heterogeneous disease by which several risk
factors can contribute to the onset and progression of the disease. The most common risk factors
include advanced age, gender (female > male), obesity, inactive lifestyle, prior joint injuries, and
genetic predisposition [5]. The main clinical symptoms of OA include usage-related joint pain,
morning- or inactivity-related stiﬀness, locomotion restriction, and mild swelling around the joint.
These symptoms tend to progress over time rather than to appear suddenly [6].
The clinical symptoms of OA arise due to structural changes of the entire synovial joint. This
includes breakdown and loss of the articular cartilage, osteophyte formation, meniscal damage,
bone sclerosis, inﬂammation of the synovial membrane, also called synovitis, and thickening of
the joint capsule (Fig.1.1) [7]. OA can aﬀect any synovial joint, however, it mainly aﬀects the
weight-bearing joints such as the knees, hips, ankles, and spine, but also hands are aﬀected [8]. For
decades, OA has been deﬁned as a "wear and tear" disease, but recently it has been acknowledged
by the Osteoarthritis Research Society International (OARSI) that the pathogenesis and progression
of OA may also be inﬂuenced by joint inﬂammation [9].

1.1.1
Diagnosis and Management of Osteoarthritis
Commonly, the diagnosis of OA is based on the clinical symptoms, though it is supported by
radiographic classiﬁcation to identify and grade OA. The standard Kellgren-Lawrence (KL) grading
system is centered around an x-ray scoring system used to classify the severity of knee OA by
ﬁve grades where the highest grade indicates severe symptoms of OA [11]. The KL grading
system is primarily related to bone alterations based on the evaluation of osteophytes, bone
deformity and joint space narrowing [12]. These advanced bone alterations are signs of relatively
late-stage OA development. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) may be applied for detection of
the pathological changes in the early stages of OA, which is believed to involve damage of the soft
tissue (e.g. articular cartilage) [13]. Preceding MRI detection, molecular alterations of the joint can
be monitored by serological biomarkers. This enables early detection of pathological alterations
of the joint long before the onset of clinical symptoms, which can prompt diagnosis of OA and
potentially improve treatment and outcome for the patient.
Currently, there is no cure for OA. However, palliative treatments exist to manage the symptoms
by relieving pain and maintaining the joint function. The symptomatic available treatments
include non-pharmacological and pharmacological management, where for mild symptoms non-
pharmacological treatments should be considered ﬁrst [14]. The recommended approach begins
with non-pharmacological treatments, which includes education in disease management, physical
activity to improve joint mobility and ﬂexibility, and weight management for obese individuals.

osteoarthritis drugs (DMOADs).

1.2
The Synovial Joint
Joints are located in the body where bones come together and are structurally categorised into three
main types of joints: the ﬁbrous, cartilaginous, and synovial joints. This study focuses on the latter.
The synovial joint, also named diarthrodial joint, is the most common type of joint on mammals. It
is found in several joints of the body, including the knee. The synovial joint is characterised by the
articular cavity, where the articulating bones are covered with articular cartilage. This allows for
smooth, nearly frictionless sliding of the bones in the joint. Few synovial joints, including the knee,
also contain an articular disc (meniscus) between the articulating bones, which provides shock
absorption and serve smooth load transfer from femur to tibia.
The space between the articulating bones is ﬁlled with synovial ﬂuid, which supplies the cartilage
with nutrients and creates a lubricating cushion. The joint is surrounded by a synovial membrane
creating a reservoir for the synovial ﬂuid. The ﬁbrous joint capsule, also known as the patella,
and the ligaments are attached to the bone and provide stability and strength to the joint (Fig.1.3).
Altogether, this characteristic structure of the synovial joint allows the bones to perform large and
smooth movements, increasing the joint mobility.

1.3
Articular Cartilage
The articular cartilage degradation is acknowledged as one of the major hallmarks of OA. However,
the cartilage is essential for proper functioning of the synovial joint. It consists of connective tissue
which is specialised in protecting the joint from mechanical loading. Thus, the articular cartilage
provides a smooth, load-bearing, lubricated surface for load transfer with minimum friction [18].
The thickness of the cartilage varies between the individual joints and mammalian species. In
human knees, the mean cartilage thickness of the femur is 1.82 ± 0.18 mm, unlike the bovine knee,
where the mean cartilage thickness is 1.32 ± 0.13 mm [19]. The articular cartilage comprises hyaline
cartilage which is the most abundant type of cartilage in the human body. Hyaline cartilage mainly
consists of the extracellular matrix (ECM), which is largely composed of water, type II collagen,
and proteoglycans. Furthermore, chondrocytes are embedded in the matrix as the only resident
cell type in the articular cartilage. Unlike most tissues, articular cartilage is avascular, alymphatic
and aneural which gives the tissue limited capacity for healing and repair when injured [18].
The distribution and arrangement of cartilage components are not uniform throughout the tissue.
Cartilage is divided into four zones: the superﬁcial, transitional, deep and calciﬁed cartilage
zones. These are characterised by diﬀerent collagen orientation, proteoglycan distribution and
organisation of chondrocytes. The Superﬁcial Zone is located at the surface of the articular
cartilage towards the joint cavity and is the thinnest cartilage zone occupying 10-20% of the
entire thickness of articular cartilage. Here, the matrix is rich in collagen and water but poor
in proteoglycans. Furthermore, the collagen ﬁbres are arranged parallel to the articular surface
providing the greatest tensile and shear strength. Compared to the other zones, the superﬁcial
zone contains a high amount of chondrocytes, which are ﬂattened and lie parallel to the joint
surface [18, 20]. The Transitional Zone, or the middle zone, represents 40-60% of the total cartilage
volume. It contains spherical shaped chondrocytes and collagen ﬁbres arranged obliquely to the
articular surface. [18, 20]. The Deep Zone makes up to approximately 30% of the total cartilage
volume. The collagen ﬁbres are arranged radially and the interﬁbrillar space is densely ﬁlled with
proteoglycans. Among all zones, this zone contains the highest proteoglycan concentration but is
the least hydrated zone with lowest cellular content. As transitional zone, the deep zone contains
spherical shaped chondrocytes. However, here the chondrocytes are organised in columnar
orientation, aligned in parallel with the radial collagen ﬁbres and perpendicular to the joint surface.
The deep zone is providing the greatest resistance to compressive forces, given the orientation
of the collagen ﬁbrils, the high concentration of the proteoglycans and the low amount of water.
Overall, this zone is the least deformable one [18, 20]. Finally, The Calciﬁed cartilage zone is
located on top of the subchondral bone. Here, the cartilage is securely attached by anchoring the
radial collagen ﬁbres to the subchondral bone. The calciﬁed zone contains a little number of large
rounded hypertrophic chrondrocytes [21–23]. The deep and calciﬁed cartilage zone are separated
by the tidemark, which marks the transition from the soft to the stiﬀcalciﬁed cartilage (Fig. 1.3,
right) [18, 20]. Overall, the morphology of the articular cartilage structure is very important for its
biomechanical properties and ability to withstand loading.

1.3.1
The Extracellular Matrix
The collagen ﬁbres provide the structural framework of the cartilage and are the most abundant
macromolecule in the cartilage ECM, representing about 60% of the dry weight in cartilage [24].
The cartilage ECM consists of several types of collagen. However, with 90-95% the ﬁbrillar type II
collagen is the most predominant type [24]. It is conserved between mammalian species including
the human and bovine genome and is encoded by the single gene COL2A1 [25]. Type II collagen
is accompanied by other collagen types in minor proportions such as type IV, VI, IX, X, XI, XII,
supporting the overall compressive strength of the cartilage matrix [26].
The ﬁbrillar collagen network is intertwined with highly hydrophilic proteoglycans representing
the second-largest group of macromolecules in the ECM [24]. A variety of proteoglycans exist
in the cartilage ECM such as aggrecan, decorin, biglycan and ﬁbromodulin. Aggrecan is the
largest and most predominant type of the proteoglycans [27]. Furthermore, it binds noncovalently
to hyaluronan via link protein forming large aggregating proteoglycans. Aggrecan consists of
many long sulphated glycosaminoglycan (GAG) side chains, consisting mainly of chondroitin
sulphate and keratan sulphate, which are covalently attached to the aggrecan core protein [28]. The
numerous sulphated GAGs are highly negatively charged molecules which extend out from the
protein core, and thus remains separated from one another due to the charge repulsion. The highly
polar GAGs attract water and creates a swelling pressure enabling the tissue to absorb and resist
mechanical stress [29]. Although type II collagen and proteoglycans are the main macromolecules
of the articular cartilage ECM, water is the most abundant component representing 80% of the wet
weight [18]. The large amount of water is critical for the compressive resistance of the articular
cartilage (Fig. 1.4) [18].
Altogether, the compressive strength of the collagens and the hydrophilic proteoglycans preserving
the high interstitial water content within the ECM, contribute to maintaining the unique mechanical
properties of the articular cartilage.
1.4
Articular Chondrocytes and the Cartilage Metabolism
The articular cartilage consists of chondrocytes which are resident within a lacunae. They occupy
around 1-5% of the total volume of adult articular cartilage [31]. Despite their sparse distribution,
they are responsible for the maintenance of the ECM homeostasis by synthesis and degradation
of matrix constituents such as the type II collagen and proteoglycans. To maintain the ECM
homeostasis, the chondrocytes are constantly remodelling the matrix in response to diﬀerent
stimuli from their surrounding biochemical and biomechanical environment [32].
1.4.1
Biochemical stimuli
The chondrocytes are sensitive to their surroundings and can be inﬂuenced by growth factors
and cytokines. These biochemical stimuli activate chondrocytes to initiate both anabolic and
catabolic processes to maintain cartilage homeostasis [32]. Catabolic biochemical factors include
the pro-inﬂammatory cytokines such as tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α) which has shown to
be a key degradation molecule for cartilage breakdown. TNF-α activates the chondrocytes to
secrete proteolytic enzymes including the matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs) and the disintegrin

the balance between catabolic and anabolic processes. Thus, when cartilage components are
degraded, they are replaced by newly synthesised products in healthy tissue.
Several anabolic growth factors work together to stimulate the synthesis of cartilage formation.
Insulin-like growth factor 1 (IGF-1) is considered to be one of the main anabolic growth factors
of normal cartilage and is present in the synovial ﬂuid throughout life [36]. The IGF-1 gene
is highly conserved within 25 mammalian species, including human and bovine [37, 38]. A
number of studies have emphasised the important role of IGF-1 to stimulate matrix synthesis
[39–44]. Furthermore, a study by J.A. Tyler (1989) showed that IGF-1 can reduce the proteoglycan
degradation induced by catabolic inﬂammatory cytokines in articular cartilage [45]. Therefore,
IGF-1 is considered a potential drug candidate for cartilage repair.
The liver is the primary organ responsible for production of circulating IGF-1 in response to growth
hormone (GH) stimulation. The produced IGF-1 reaches the cartilage through the synovial ﬂuid in
the joint cavity [46]. Additionally, IGF-1 can also be produced locally by the chondrocytes thus,
acting as autocrine and paracrine signalling [47–51].

IGF-1 signals through the IGF-1 receptor (IGF-IR), a tyrosine kinase receptor, localised on the
surface of the chondrocytes. The activation of IGF-IR mediates the intercellular signalling pathway
phosphatidylinositol-3 kinase Akt (PI3K)-Akt which regulates many key cellular processes such as
cell proliferation, diﬀerentiation, protein synthesis, and cell survival [52]. The IGF-1 signalling is
partly controlled by the IGF-binding proteins (IGFBPs), as IGF-1 only is bioactive in its free form.
IGF-1 has higher binding aﬃnity to the IGFBPs than the IGF-IR and hence, IGFBPs negatively
regulate the amount of IGF-1 available to bind IGF-IR. IGFBPs prolong the half-life of the IGF-1 by
protecting IGF-1 from degradation, and functions as IGF-1 carriers by transporting them to the
target tissue [53]. Six diﬀerent types of IGFBPs are known IGFBP-1 to -6 [54], and chondrocytes
most abundantly secrete IGFBP-3 [55]. The IGFBPs can act as stores of IGFs within the matrix [56].
Although IGF-1 and IGFBPs have been widely studied, it still remains unclear exactly how their
presence contributes to the synthesis of cartilage ECM.
1.4.2
Biomechanical stimuli
The articular cartilage functions under daily mechanical loading which is considered a critical
regulator of the cartilage metabolism. Physiological applied loading has shown to stimulate
matrix synthesis [57–62]. The physiological pressure in a healthy knee is highly debated however,
it is believed to range between approximately 1 and 6 MPa [63, 64].
Although, loading is
believed to be important for the development and maintenance of cartilage homeostasis, cartilage
responds diﬀerently depending on the intensity of the mechanical loading. Hence, mechanical
hyperphysiological loading may cause joint injuries resulting in cartilage degradation. In a study
by Torzilli et al. (1999), the loading impact on cartilage was examined and a critical threshold stress
of 15 to 20 MPa was observed causing damage to cartilage structure including cell death [65]. It has
also been shown in ex vivo study that supra-physiological loads on 20 MPa leads to overexpression
of catabolic enzymes [66]. Furthermore, Hinterwimmer et al. (2004) demonstrated that reduced
loading in the human knee for 7 weeks resulted in signiﬁcantly thinning of the articular cartilage
[67]. These previous studies indicate that abnormal loading may promote unhealthy cartilage
tissue turnover alterations.
During daily locomotive activities, the cartilage is subjected to dynamic loading also called cyclic
loading. Under dynamic loading, the pressure causes the interstitial ﬂuid to gradually be squeezed
out of ECM (also called the creep phase) and the cartilage gradually looses its height [68]. After
numbers of load cycles, a steady state of cartilage deformation is reached, where nearly all ﬂuid
is squeezed out of the matrix [69]. This deformation provides stability during locomotion by
increasing the contact area of the joint, which reduces the joint contact pressure [70]. Hereafter,
when no loading is being applied to the cartilage, the interstitial ﬂuid ﬂows back into the matrix
and the cartilage recovers to its original height with unchanged mechanical properties. This phase
is called the creep-recovery phase. It is not well understood how the creep and creep-recovery
phase aﬀect the articular cartilage homeostasis [65]. However, it is believed that the interstitial
ﬂuid ﬂow through ECM created by locomotion is important for chondrocyte metabolism. Due to
the avascular nature of cartilage, the ﬂuid ﬂow helps transporting and distributing nutrients and
growth factors to the chondrocytes [71, 72].
It has been proposed that the chondrocyte membrane possesses mechanosensitive ion channels
which are activated in response to mechanical loading [73–76]. The mechanosensitive Ca2+ perme-
able ion channels, PIEZO1 and transient receptor potential vanilloid 4 (TRPV4), have shown to be

The progressive ECM degradation in OA involves type II collagen and proteoglycans and leads to
concomitant structural and functional changes [24]. In normal cartilage, the superﬁcial layer is
intact while in OA, the breakdown of cartilage leads to an irregular surface aﬀecting its mechanical
properties (Fig. 1.5) [77, 78].

1.5.1
Chondrocytes in Osteoarthritis
One of the ﬁrst signs of OA is the change in the chondrocyte phenotype toward a hypertrophic phe-
notype [21, 24]. During the terminal diﬀerentiation to chondrocyte hypertrophy, the chondrocytes
increase in size with an enlarged lacuna [86]. They start synthesising collagen type X, a collagen
component present in the calciﬁed cartilage matrix [87], and producing ECM degrading proteases
such as MMP13 [88–90]. Furthermore, hypertrophic chondrocytes can undergo apoptosis, leading
to endochondral ossiﬁcation where cartilage is replaced by bone [22, 91].

1.6.1
Translational Biomarkers
In this project, translational biomarkers developed by Nordic Bioscience were used in the ex vivo
models, bovine cartilage explant (BEX) model and compression model, to characterise the type
II collagen turnover. The advantage of using these translational biomarkers, is that they can be
measured in supernatant samples harvested from ex vivo model and can be compared to clinical
serum samples. Furthermore, the translational biomarkers are neoepitope-speciﬁc and reﬂect
metabolic processing of type II collagen, which are used in this thesis to characterise the structural
turnover of cartilage.
PRO-C2
The type II collagen formation is quantiﬁed by measuring the pro-peptide fragment biomarker,
PRO-C2, which is released during synthesis. In the synthesis, three identical α1(II) polypeptide
chains are assembled into the formation of procollagen, which contains a N- and C-terminal with
a helical domain. The procollagen is secreted into the extracellular space where the terminal
pro-peptides are cleaved oﬀby N-proteinases and C-proteinases to form the tropocollagen. Fi-
nally, tropocollagens are packed into quarter-staggered collagen ﬁbrils [96]. As the N-terminal
pro-peptide undergoes alternative splicing, the cleavage by the N-proteinases leaves a speciﬁc
neoepitope peptide sequence, QDVRQPG, of the beta splice variant of type II procollagen (PIIBNP),
which is detected by a monoclonal antibody in the PRO-C2 assay (Fig. 1.6) [40].

Aim of the Project
Although OA is the most prevalent arthritic disease worldwide, there are no disease modifying
treatments for people suﬀering from OA. A major hallmark of OA pathogenesis is the progressive
degradation of the joint tissues, especially the articular cartilage. Preclinical ﬁndings have proven
that IGF-1 is a strong candidate for articular cartilage repair and may be a potential target for novel
therapeutic options for OA management. However, when novel therapies are tested in preclinical
models of OA, their predictive utility is often questioned because their preclinical eﬃciency is
poorly translated in clinical trials. To enhance the translational value of preclinical, translational
biomarkers are suggested in order to bridge the gap between preclinical and clinical studies. Given
the biomechanical function of articular cartilage, a former study has tested IGF-1 together with
mechanical loading in an ex vivo bovine cartilage model. The study showed that the combined
stimuli of IGF-1 and mechanical loading may have an even greater anabolic eﬀect on the articular
cartilage formation compared to each stimulus alone. Consequently, to test if this additive eﬀect
can be translated into clinical trials, the combined eﬀect of IGF-1 and compression is examined
by translational biomarkers. This thesis assesses whether a translational ex vivo bovine cartilage
model can demonstrate the additive eﬀect of IGF-1 and intermittent dynamic compression on the
cartilage formation.
Hence, the project hypothesis states: Combining IGF-1 treatment and intermittent dynamic
compression in translational ex vivo bovine cartilage model increases the type II collagen
formation compared to each stimuli alone.
The hypothesis is examined by the following project aim: To investigate the combined eﬀect of
IGF-1 and long-term intermittent dynamic compression on the type II collagen in an ex vivo
bovine cartilage model. The cartilage remodelling is be quantiﬁed by translational turnover
biomarkers. Moreover, histology is be performed to support the experimental ﬁndings.
The project aim is divided into following sub-aims:
I) Investigate potential additive eﬀect of IGF-1 and dynamic compression. Hereof, optimising
the setup of the ex-vivo model with IGF-1 and compression:
1) Investigate the IGF-1 concentrations relevant in combination with dynamic compression.
2) Determine the dynamic compression settings optimal for investigating an additive eﬀect
of compression and IGF-1.
II) Furthermore, it became clear during the project that another sub-project needed to be
performed to limit an emerging problem with fungal contamination, hence a third sub-aim
was included:
1) Optimise the ex vivo culture medium with anti-fungal agent.

The mechanical compression settings for the compression study were selected to mimic physio-
logical loading of the human knee in low-impact situations such as regular walking [101, 102].
The settings are currently considered as standard settings which have already been used in
the compression model for other studies at Nordic Bioscience [99]. Explants were subjected
to unconﬁned dynamic mechanical compression performed in a sine wave of 1 Hz frequency
(1 cycle/sec) with applied compressive force of between approximately 0.06 MPa to 1 MPa per
explant performed with 20 min. of duration (1200 cycles) (Fig. 3.3). Taken into account that the
natural magnitude of static loading observed in human knees ranges approximately from 1 to 6
MPa when performing mild to moderate activity [64], 1 MPa is considered light loading stress.
The maximum loading capacity of the machine is approximately 24 MPa, and 24 MPa loading
distributed between 24-wells results in 1 MPa maximum loading per well. If greater magnitude is
desired, the number of replicates or treatment must be reduced. From the ﬁrst loading cycle, the
magnitude is slowly increasing until it reaches maximum loading of 1 MPa to protect the cartilage
surface from injury. The performed loading is controlled by WinTest® software package, which has
a tuning system with a TuneIQ algorithm that takes into account the ﬂexibility of cartilage tissue
when getting compressed. The applied loading force is measured by one channel which indirectly
controls the displacement channel. Contemporary with the explants receive increased cyclic load-
ing, the interstitial ﬂuid is slowly compressed out of the matrix, reducing the thickness of the explant.

3.5. METABOLIC ACTIVITY ASSESSMENT
25
3.5
Metabolic Activity Assessment
The AlamarBlue assay was used to measure the metabolic activity of the cells and thus, indirectly
quantify the cell viability of the cartilage explants during the culture period and at the termination.
The AlamarBlue reagent consists of the active ingredient, resazurin, which is non-toxic and a
blue non-ﬂuorescent molecule. Additionally, resazurin is a cell permeable compound and when
entering the cells, the reducing environment within healthy living cells reduces resazurin into the
red highly ﬂuorescent compound resoruﬁn (Fig. 3.6). The ﬂuorescence intensity is proportional to
the metabolic activity. A continuously increased ﬂuorescence is an indicator of the viable cells
reducing resazurin into resoruﬁn [108]. As a thump rule, if explants exhibited low metabolic
activity under 2000 ﬂuorescence intensity units which continuously decreased throughout the
study, the explants were excluded from the experiment. The AlamarBlue assay was applied
because it allows the measurement of the cells inside the matrix.
The explants were incubated with 10 % AlamarBlue in culture medium for 3 hours at 37 °C,
5 % CO2. Subsequently, conditioned media were transferred to a black 96-well plate and the
ﬂuorescence intensity was detected by using SpectraMax® ELISA ﬂuorescent reader (Molecular
Devices) with excitation at 540 nm and emission at 590 nm. Four AlamarBlue control wells without
explants were included to adjust for background. The metabolic activity was measured once
weekly in all experiments. Appendix section B4 is referred to for detailed protocol description.

Competitive ELISA
PRO-C2 was measured by indirect competitive ELISA and C2M by direct competitive ELISA.
The key diﬀerence between these two types of competitive ELISAs is that indirect ELISA uses
both primary capture antibody and secondary detection antibody. Whereas for direct ELISA, the
capturing antibody is also the detection antibody hence, a secondary detection antibody is not
used in direct ELISA.
At Nordic Bioscience, the competitive ELISAs were performed in a streptavidin pre-coated mi-
crotiter plate with 96-wells. The streptavidin-coated plate was incubated with the biotinylated
antigen for 30 min. at 18-22 °C. The biotinylated antigen is speciﬁc for the primary antibody.
After incubation, the biotinylated antigens that did not bind to the plate were washed oﬀ. The
ELISA standard curve was generated by serial dilutions of one known concentration of puriﬁed
target antigen in order to quantify antigen concentration in supernatant samples. The peptide
controls of known concentrations, CO1 and CO2, were added as plate controls. Concurrently,
the thawed stored supernatant samples from culturing plates and the monoclonal antibodies
speciﬁc for the antigen were added to the plate and incubated for 2 hours at 18-22 °C (PRO-C2) or
20 hours at 4 °C (C2M). Within this time, the sample antigens from supernatant and the coated
antigen "competes" for the monoclonal antibody binding. The soluble complexes formed by sample
antigen binding to antibody were washed oﬀand only antibodies binding to the antigen-coated
wells were detected. The higher concentration of antigen in the sample, the more antibodies
bind to the sample antigen and a reduced amount of antibody will be available for binding the

3.7
Glycosaminoglycans Assay
The glycosaminoglycan assay is a rapid method for quantifying the amount of sulphated gly-
coaminoglycans released from the cartilage into the supernatant. The assay is based on thiazine
Dimethylmethylene Blue (DMMB) which instantly interacts with sulfate group of GAG allowing
rapid detection of GAG in supernatant. DMMB exhibits metachromasia in presence of sulphated
GAGs inducting a colour change (from blue to pink) and the absorption peak shifts from about 630
nm to about 530 nm [110].
The ELISA standard curve was generated by serial dilutions of Chondroitin sulphate. The prepared
standard dilutions and supernatant samples were added to a 96-well plate. The DMMB dye
solution was added and absorbance immediately measured at 605 nm in VersaMax® Microplate
Reader (Molecular Devices). Appendix section B7 is referred to for detailed protocol description.

3.8.1
Tissue Preparation
After ﬁxation, each explant was placed into an individual labelled cassette. The cassettes were
transferred into the tissue processor machine (Sakura Tissue-Tek VIP 5 Jr) where the explants were
inﬁltrated in paraﬃn wax after a series of dehydration and ethanol clearance steps. These included:
1) Dehydration with 96% ethanol for 90 min. with no temperature adjustment, repeated thrice. 2)
Ethanol clearance with toluene for 90 min. with no temperature adjustment, repeated twice. 3)
Ethanol clearance with toluene for 90 min. at 60 °C. 4) Inﬁltration with paraﬃn wax for 90 min. at
60 °C, repeated thrice.
After the paraﬃn inﬁltration, the cassettes were placed on a heating block to enable the removal
of the explants from the cassettes. Each explant was positioned with the superﬁcial articular
cartilage and subchondral bone side edgeways towards the cutting surface to ensure visualisation
of the diﬀerent cartilage layers. In this position, the explants were separately embedded into
paraﬃn blocks. The cooled paraﬃn blocks with explants were cut into 5 µm thick sections using a
Microtome HM 355/360 machine (Thermo Fisher Scientiﬁc) and transferred to a cold water bath
followed by a 50 °C warm water bath to allow the explant sections to unfold. Each section was
placed onto a labelled Superfrost Plus microscope slide and transferred to a hot plate for 30 min.
The slides were incubated at 60 °C for 1 hour to remove excess paraﬃn, and further incubated
overnight at 37 °C to ensure that the sections completely adhered to the glass slides. Until staining,
the slides were kept in closed storage box at 4 °C.

IGF-1 Titration Study
A preliminary IGF-1 titration study was performed in the BEX model to determine which IGF-1
concentrations to apply in the compression model.
When selecting the optimal IGF-1 concentrations, it was considered that IGF-1 is a stronger inducer
of type II collagen formation compared to mechanical compression. Therefore, when combining
IGF-1 and mechanical compression, the increased type II collagen formation should be an additive
result of both stimuli. It is not known how the combined IGF-1 and mechanical compression
stimuli lead to the potential additive eﬀect on the type II collagen formation. Thus, it cannot
be precluded that IGF-1 and mechanical loading may stimulate the type II collagen formation
through the same signalling pathway. To avoid that IGF-1 stimulus dominates the stimulus by
mechanical compression, the IGF-1 concentrations were chosen to allow the presence of the eﬀect
of compression in combination with IGF-1.
The metabolic activity was measured on day 0, 7, 14, and 21. If explants exhibited low metabolic
activity under 2000 ﬂuorescence intensity units which continuously decreased throughout the
experiment, they were excluded from the experiment. However, it has previously been observed
from studies at Nordic Bioscience that O+T treatment may decrease the metabolic activity. All
explants exhibited a stable metabolic activity above the 2000, except from O+T treated explants
(Fig. 4.2).

CHAPTER5
Discussion
This project aims to elucidate the proposed additive anabolic interaction between IGF-1 and
dynamic compression previously demonstrated in Bonassar et al. (2001). IGF-1 and dynamic
compression are biochemical and biomechanical factors believed to contribute to the regulation of
articular cartilage in health and disease. The potential additive eﬀect of IGF-1 and compression
was tested in the compression model and analysed with translational biomarkers to characterise
the remodelling of type II collagen, the main collagen of articular cartilage.
5.1
The Ex Vivo Model
Diﬀerent preclinical models exist to study the cartilage metabolism both in vitro, ex vivo and in vivo.
In vitro experiments with cultured isolated chondrocytes are easy, inexpensive models, well-suited
for examining a speciﬁc cytokine or signalling pathway. However, since OA is believed to be a
disease of the matrix, the in vitro model is unsatisfactory for studying the mediated alterations of the
matrix. Instead, the ex vivo model was selected for this study, where cells are held in their natural
matrix but outside the host, which is believed to be a better ﬁt for studying matrix remodelling.
The explant model oﬀers the ability to elucidate alterations of the ECM mediated by chondrocytes
embedded in their natural environment with preserved matrix macromolecules [111]. The study
of a heterogeneous matrix provides a better representation of the natural environment, as in vivo.
This may provide more reliable investigation of the chondrocyte-mediated alterations to the ECM
in response to diﬀerent stimuli, which is very important when testing potential therapies [94].
The cartilage explant model oﬀers visualisation of the chondrocyte distribution, preservation of
chondrocyte phenotype and the cartilage ECM composition. The in vivo models cover experiments
in live animals where the cells are maintained in their natural environment and host. The in vivo
models are much more complex models compared to ex vivo, where the experimental conditions
can be diﬃcult to control. Furthermore, in vivo models includes the ethical aspect of using living
animals [111].
When testing a potential OA drug in ex vivo models, it is suggested to use OA human cartilage
biopsies removed from the knee joint during replacement.
However, due to the extensive
degradation of OA cartilage the amount of cartilage left for experiments is limited. It is almost
impossible to get healthy human cartilage. Instead, healthy young bovine tissue is used which
provides a number of advantages: 1) Bovine cartilage is easily accessible and allows harvesting
of up to 50 uniform cartilage explants per joint which are of high homogeneous quality. 2) The
bovine cartilage thickness, morphometry, and zonality is similar to humans [112].
Although, there a beneﬁts of using healthy bovine tissue, it cannot be known whether the anabolic
stimulation with IGF-1 observed in healthy bovine tissue can be seen in OA human tissue. IGF-1
has poor anabolic eﬃcacy in OA cartilage despite the increased concentration of IGF-1 found in the

by the increased production of IGFBPs in OA [85]. This negatively regulates IGF-I signaling in

Since the articular cartilage tissue functions under daily loading, the ex vivo model has been

5.2
Investigating the Combined Eﬀect of IGF-1 and Long-term Intermittent
Dynamic Compression on Type II Collagen
Throughout all studies in this project, the chondrocyte viability was assessed indirectly by
measuring the metabolic activity once weekly to ensure that the cells were alive throughout the
study. The metabolic activity was kept stable for 21 days of culturing. Therefor, the modulations in
biomarker secretions observed in the studies are not a results of changes in chondrocyte viability.
To test the combined eﬀect, stimulus from both IGF-1 and compression needs to be observed in
the study. The IGF-1 concentrations were optimised until IGF-1 showed a signiﬁcant eﬀect on all
IGF-1 concentrations. Furthermore, the compression settings also needed to be optimised due to
lack of eﬀect in the ﬁrst setups. Several compression settings were considered to be changed, such
as increasing the amplitude above 1 MPa. However, when enhancing the amplitude, the number
of treatments or replicates needed to be reduced due to the limited capacity of the compression
instrument. In the study by Bonassar et al. (2001), the dynamic compression was performed
for 48 hours, which resulted in an additive eﬀect of IGF-1 and compression [114]. Thus, it was
suggested that changing the dynamic compression duration may increase the type II collagen
formation. Hence, the loading duration was increased to 60 min. 3 times a week. The changed
duration compression setting did not appear to increase the type II collagen formation, therefore
the compression settings was returned to 20 min. with loading 5 times a week instead of the
previous 3 times a week.
The eﬀect of compression appeared after increasing the number of compression runs per week.
Since the eﬀect of compression on cartilage metabolism is not fully understood, it is unclear
why increasing the weekly number of compression runs had an eﬀect in the study compared to
increasing the loading duration. One explanation could be that more PRO-C2 is accumulated in
the supernatant in response to compression. In previous setup with 60 min. loading 3 times a
week, the harvested supernatant contained accumulated PRO-C2 from 1 compression run, whereas
in the new setup with 20 min. loading 5 times a week, the supernatant contained accumulated
PRO-C2 from 2 compression runs. Another explanation may be the increased number of creep
and creep-recovery phases in the explant. It is not understood how the cartilage deformation
aﬀects the behaviour of the chondrocytes, but with more creep and creep-recovery phases, new
interstitial ﬂuid ﬂows into the explants, which may supply the chondrocytes with new nutrients.
Furthermore, since no diﬀerent eﬀect was observed for explants receiving compression between 20
and 60 min. of duration, the activation of mechanosensitive receptors in the chondrocytes may
not be as dependent on the duration of compression as they are dependent on the presence of

An increased variation was observed between the explants from the same treatment group in study
4, especially in groups receiving compression including the compression control group. Although,

low variation between the explants. Due to the architecture of cartilage, the organisation and
number of chondrocytes and the matrix composition varies throughout the tissue. Furthermore,
the articular cartilage also varies between cows. Therefore, it is recommended to normalise datasets
to baseline to correct for biological variance and more directly assess the treatment eﬀect, which
also allows better comparison between the experiments. Because of the explant variation, the
replicate number for each treatment group was doubled from 6 to 12 replicates to strengthen the
study results.
After increasing the number of replicates to 12 explants per treatment group, the individual eﬀects
of IGF-1 and compression were still present throughout the study. On day 21, an additive eﬀect
was observed for treatment groups receiving either 4 ng/mL or 100 ng/mL IGF-1, combined with
compression. The mechanism by which IGF-1 directs the matrix redistribution and the role of
dynamic compression in this process, is not yet understood. Bonassar et al. (2001) suggested
that dynamic compression enhances the eﬀect of IGF-1 by a compression-induced ﬂuid ﬂow,
contributing to the transport of IGF-1 through the cartilage matrix which increases the access of
IGF-1 to the chondrocytes [114]. This is supported by a study performed by Huang and Gu, (2006)
who demonstrated that dynamic compression enhanced the diﬀusion of large solutes, such as
growth factors, within cartilaginous tissues [71]. Based on these results, it may be that compression
increases the diﬀusion of ﬂuid ﬂow with IGF-1 into the matrix, which may accelerate the stimulation
of the chondrocytes. Furthermore, Bonassar et al. (2001) suggested that dynamic compression may
ameliorate the eﬃcacy of IGFs by enhancing the release of bound IGF-1 from the cartilage matrix
[114]. Since the chondrocytes are embedded into their native retained ECM when stimulated
with compression, it is possible that stimulating molecules bound to the matrix are released from
the matrix. Thus, it is not known how much of the type II collagen formation can be attributed
to the treatment with exogenous stimulating molecules. One of these molecules may be IGF-1.
Therefore, when treating with IGF-1 in the compression studies, it is not clear how big a propor-
tion of the type II collagen formation that is solely a results of the stimulation with exogenous IGF-1.
5.2.1
Investigating the Glycosaminoglycan Release Into the Supernatant
The aim of this thesis is to investigate the anabolic eﬀect of IGF-1 and compression, analysed
with biomarkers characterising the type II collagen remodelling. Since the loss of GAG is another
hallmark of OA [115], the eﬀect of IGF-1 and compression on the GAG degradation was investigated.
IGF-1 is considered as an anabolic growth factor and it is not expected to stimulate a catabolic
response. IGF-1 treatment without compression seemed not to activate a catabolic response as it did
not increase the GAG release, which supports IGF-1 being an anabolic stimulus. In previous studies
from Nordic Bioscience, where GAG release has been measured for explants receiving compression,
it has been shown that compression may increase the GAG remodelling. In this project, compression
showed to increase the GAG release but when results were normalised to day 0, the eﬀect of

5.3
Validation Method
Histological staining was conducted in order to verify if the superﬁcial cartilage layer was kept
intact after performing mechanical compression. Furthermore, it reveals any microscopic changes
to the chondrocyte phenotype. The proteoglycan/collagen content of explants was visualised
by Safranin-O/Fast Green staining. The loaded explants did not show any signs of surface
damage when exposed to mechanical compression. In the compression model, the cartilage
metabolism is studied in healthy bovine cartilage. Therefore, it was important to conﬁrm the the
mechanical compression did not injure the cartilage. In OA tissue, damage to the superﬁcial layer
is observed which is believed to aﬀect the cartilage metabolism [32]. The visualised proteoglycan
content in the cartilage matrix seemed to be largely unaﬀected by IGF-1 treatment and dynamic
compression. The chondrocytes treated with 100 ng/mL IGF-1 showed enlarged cell size in the deep
cartilage zone, which may be an indication of hypertrophic morphology. Chondrocytes in diseased
cartilage are characterised by the development of a hypertrophic phenotype, associated with
expression of pro-inﬂammatory mediators, and including the expression of collagen X [22, 23]. The
potential phenotypic shift of chondrocytes when treated with 100 ng/mL IGF has previously been
demonstrated in a study by Pinping et al. (2013) from Nordic Bioscience [100]. Here, the mRNA
expression of collagen X was increased but also the PRO-C2 levels were enhanced. Normally in the
human knee environment, primarily the superﬁcial zone is exposed to IGF-1 stimulation from the
synovial ﬂuid, where in this model all cartilage zones are exposed to high concentrations of IGF-1.
Therefore, the potential chondrocyte phenotypic shift in the deep zone may be due to increased
IGF-1 exposure in the deeper layers contributing to abnormal stimulation.
Observing only hypertrophic chondrocytes for the highest IGF-1 100 ng/mL concentration but not
for 4 ng/mL may indicate that there seems to be an optimal IGF-1 dose in terms of not triggering
the phenotypic shift. In general, this model only focuses on exogenous stimulation of chondrocytes
with one growth factor, IGF-1. In the natural environment of the knee, several growth factors work
together to keep the cartilage homoeostasis by strictly regulatiion [116]. This model represents
a very simple model compared to the complex interactions between growth factors which takes
place in the knee environment. Thus, it is not clear whether the chondrocytes would shift into a
hypertrophic phenotype when stimulated with 100 ng/mL IGF-1 in combination with other growth
factors which could be tested in in vivo models.

5.4
Improvement of the Ex Vivo Culture Medium with Anti-fungal Agent
The culture medium of the compression model was optimised with the anti-fungal agent, Fungizone,
due to repeated fungal contamination in the ex vivo culture. Fungizone interacts with the cell
membrane by its aﬃnity for sterols, particularly ergosterols, and kills fungi [117]. Fungizone has not
been applied before in the culture medium at Nordic Bioscience, and thus, to ensure that Fungizone
did not aﬀect the metabolic activity of the chondrocytes including the collagen remodelling, an
optimisation study was established to test whether Fungizone aﬀected the experimental outcome.
Based on the results from section 4.3, the metabolic activity of the chondrocytes including the type
II collagen formation and degradation did not seem to be aﬀected by Fungizone of 2.5 µg/mL. The
optimisation study was carried out for 14 days, whereas the normal setup for compression study is
performed for 21 days. In principle, it cannot be ruled out that Fungizone does not aﬀect the study
outcome on day 21, which should be kept ind mind. Furthermore, Fungizone did not appear to
interfere with the PRO-C2 assay which is of high importance because it is the pivotal analysing
tool to test the hypothesis of the project.
The applied Fungizone concentration of 2.5 µg/mL was the recommended concentration from the
supplier for maintenance of anti-fungal cell cultures [107]. Therefore, it is important to keep in
mind, that the study investigates the inﬂuence of Fungizone at a particular concentration selected
based on its ability to avoid fungal infections and not its toxicity level for mammalian cells. The
study does not assess whether Fungizone is toxic for mammalian cells in general. Since Fungizone
is thought to act by its binding aﬃnity for sterols [118], it may be toxic to mammalian cells in
higher concentrations than the recommended. Therefore, it was decided to only include Fun-
gizone in the culturing medium in the washing steps performed once a week for minimum exposure.
5.5
Increasing the Translational Value of Preclinical Results when
Combining IGF-1 and Dynamic Compression
To bridge the gap between preclinical and clinical trials for OA drug development, Nordic
Bioscience has developed a translational ex vivo cartilage model with mechanical loading. In this
project, this model was used to assess the potential additive eﬀect of IGF-1 and compression,
which may in the future be translated into clinical studies to guide decision-making during the
development of OA therapeutics.
When Bonassar et al. (2001) demonstrated the additive eﬀect of IGF-1 and dynamic compression,
the cartilage synthesis was examined by [35]S-sulfate and [3]H-proline labeling, visualising the
sulphate-incorporation into GAGs and the hydroxyproline-incorporation into collagen, respectively.
The radiolabeling incorporation method is widely used for analysing the synthesis of cartilage
[59] however, it is relatively unspeciﬁc compared to measuring neo-epitope biomarkers. The
measured hydroxyproline incorporation includes synthesis of many types of collagen. However,
by measuring the speciﬁc collagen turnover neoepitope peptides applied in this project, it provides
information about a certain biochemical process in a known collagen. Although, type II collagen is
the most dominant collagen in hyaline cartilage, it cannot be known with certainty which type of
collagen is synthesised when applying radiolabeling as a measure for collagen formation. When
developing OA therapies for cartilage repair, it is crucial to verify that the cartilage is regenerated
with native type II collagen for proper mechanical function of the cartilage. In a former study, the
52
CHAPTER 5. DISCUSSION
functional repair of cartilage defects failed, because the treatment contributed to the formation
of ﬁbrotic tissue which consists of Type I collagen of which is mechanically inferior to native
type II collagen in terms of cartilage function [119]. Hereof, it is important to ensure that type
II collagen is produced when regenerating ECM tissue. Besides the biomarker speciﬁcity, the
detected neo-epitope sequence is conserved among several species including humans, which
improves the clinical translation.
Most ex vivo models applying dynamic compression are short-term studies e.g. in Bonassar et
al. (2001), which was performed for 48 hours with constant dynamic compression. In terms of
clinical relevance, it is more suitable to analyse the cartilage remodelling in response to drug
over a longer period of time. When a new drug is tested on healthy subjects or OA patients, the
long-term eﬀects of the drug will be investigated. In OA, cartilage degradation occurs gradually
over a long-term period and it is most likely that cartilage regeneration will involve long-term
treatment. Therefore, testing a potential new OA drug with long-term compression may increase
the likelihood of showing the similar eﬀects observed in clinical trials.
In the early stage OA, patients are already recommended physical activity by daily walking, but
with limited understanding of how the mechanical loading beneﬁts the patients, it cannot be known
whether it may inﬂuence the eﬀect of a new drug. Therefore, long-term dynamic mechanical
loading should be implemented in ex vivo models when testing a potential new drug. By neglecting
the presence of loading which is naturally occurring in the human knee, it may create a blind spot
when testing novel OA therapies. The mechanical loading applied in the ex vivo needs to reﬂect
loading dynamics which is already seen in human knees when OA patients are walking. Previous
studies have shown, that the choice of loading is critical in terms of improving the cartilage
regeneration. Static and dynamic loading have been investigated, both naturally occurring in
the knee, but showed to aﬀect the cartilage remodelling in diﬀerent ways. For example, when
considering the ﬂuid ﬂow generated by mechanical compression, static or dynamic compression
inﬂuenced the growth factor transport into the matrix with diﬀerent velocity. Bonassar et al.
(2000) demonstrated, that static compression promoted a slower transport diﬀusion of IGF-1
[120] compared to the IGF-1 transport diﬀusion by dynamic compression [114]. Furthermore,
in terms of matrix synthesis, studies have shown that static loading decreases matrix synthesis
[58, 62, 121, 122]. Whereas dynamic loading has showed stimulation of matrix synthesis [57–62].
These ﬁndings emphasise the importance of the choice of loading. For example, in terms of
stimulating matrix synthesis, dynamic compression would be recommended. In other studies,
healthy bovine explants have instead been injured with extreme static loading to promote OA-like
conditions [103, 123].
Besides selecting the type of loading relevant for the study, the compression model of this project
uses intermittent dynamic loading. Hereby, the cartilage explants can return back to their original
thickness before being exposed to dynamic loading again. In Bonassar et al. (2001), the bovine
cartilage explants are exposed continuously to dynamic loading for 48 hours [114] while in this
project, the explants are exposed to intermittent compression 20 min. 5 times a week. In a clinical
study by Farrokhi et al. (2017), patients with symptomatic OA were divided into two groups
of walking exercises with either continuous or interval walking with rest. The study showed,
that continuous walking resulted in elevated knee pain, whereas interval walking did not lead to
increased knee pain of the OA patients [124]. Hereof, this may indicate that intermittent dynamic
loading may be more relevant and beneﬁcial in terms of cartilage regeneration compared to
continuous dynamic compression.
5.5. INCREASING THE TRANSLATIONAL VALUE OF PRECLINICAL RESULTS WHEN
COMBINING IGF-1 AND DYNAMIC COMPRESSION
53
Bonassar et al. (2001) demonstrated the additive ﬁndings with IGF-1 concentration on 300 ng/mL,
which is a high IGF-1 dose compared to the clinical concentration measured to approximately 19
ng/mL of healthy subjects and approximately 88 ng/mL of OA patients found in synovial ﬂuid [46].
In this project, the additive eﬀect was seen on IGF-1 doses of 4 ng/mL and 100 ng/mL IGF-1, which
are more closely related to the observed concentrations in human knee, thus may be more clinical
relevant.
Although, the combined eﬀect of IGF-1 and dynamic compression was tested in a compression
model with higher clinical relevance, it is acknowledged that this model cannot stand on its own.
These additive ﬁndings demonstrated in this compression model needs to be supported by studies
using humans explants, animal models, and if possible human clinical studies to fully verify these
results.
The cartilage metabolism is poorly understood and more research is necessary to elucidate the
fundamental mechanisms behind the combined treatment with IGF-1 and dynamic compression
before it can be employed in the medical management of OA.
CHAPTER6
Conclusion
The aim of this thesis was to investigate whether a translational ex vivo bovine cartilage model
could be used to demonstrate the potential additive eﬀect of IGF-1 and long-term intermittent
dynamic compression on cartilage formation. The additive eﬀect of IGF-1 and compression was
examined in healthy bovine cartilage explants cultured for 21 days. Throughout these studies,
chondrocytes exhibited stable metabolic activity. The cartilage remodelling was measured by a
type II collagen neo-epitope biomarkers.
Before testing the project hypothesis, the IGF-1 concentrations and the dynamic mechanical
compression settings were optimised by several experiments. After successful optimisation, this
study demonstrated:
• IGF-1 signiﬁcantly increased type II collagen formation in cartilage explants.
• Dynamic mechanical compression signiﬁcantly increased type II collagen formation in
cartilage explants.
• The combination of IGF-1 and dynamic mechanical compression signiﬁcantly increased the
type II collagen formation with an additive eﬀect.
Thus, this conﬁrms the project hypothesis.
The cartilage explants were evaluated by histological staining revealing that explants were not
injured by mechanical compression.
Furthermore, neither IGF-1 nor dynamic compression
appeared to signiﬁcantly inﬂuence the GAG degradation.
The addition of Fungizone in the ex vivo culture medium was shown not to inﬂuence the metabolic
activity, type II collagen remodelling or PRO-C2 ELISA assay measurements. Consequently,
Fungizone was included in the washing steps once a week in the following studies where no
fungal contamination was observed.
In summery, this study conﬁrmed that an additive eﬀect of IGF-1 and compression could be
demonstrated in a translational ex vivo cartilage bovine model. This suggests that the combination
of biochemical and biomechanical stimuli may enhance the formation of cartilage more eﬀectively.
Thus, the additional eﬀect of mechanical compression should be considered when testing the eﬀect
of new OA drug candidates.

CHAPTER 6. CONCLUSION
6.1
Future Work
Future work is needed to verify and examine the ﬁndings of the project.
First, to verify the additive eﬀect of IGF-1 and dynamic compression, which was only demonstrated
once, the experiment needs to be replicated to ensure reproducibility. The PRO-C2 results can be
supported by measuring the expression level of the type II collagen gene, COL2A1, by reverse
transcription polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR) analysis. Since the type II collagen formation
is measured by PRO-C2, a pro-fragment released from procollagen during synthesis, it does not
verify whether the newly synthesised type II collagen is inserted into the matrix. Therefore, a
method to validate the amount of of type II collagen incorporated in the matrix would be optimal.
The hydroxyproline-incorporation labeling method enables the detecting of collagen incorporation
in the matrix. However, the incorporation activities do not represent synthesis of a speciﬁc collagen
type, but the synthesis of all collagens present in matrix.
Secondly, For the analysis of proteoglycan remodelling, the GAG degradation results can be
strengthened by employing the AGNx1 (aggrecanase mediated degradation of aggrecan) ELISA
assay. Here, the monoclonal antibody detects the neo-epitope, NITEGE, which is generated by
aggrecanase cleavage of aggrecan C-terminal [125]. AGNx1 was not measured during this project,
because the AGNx1 concentration was under LLOQ. To concentrate the AGNx1 biomarker, the
SpeedVac Vacuum Concentrators (Thermo Scientiﬁc) could be used, enabling AGNx1 to be in
the AGNx1 assay measuring range. Furthermore, to investigate the anabolic eﬀect of IGF-1 and
dynamic compression on aggrecan formation, expression levels of the aggrecan gene, ACAN [126],
can be measured by RT-qPCR.
Thirdly, to conﬁrm that the enlarged chondrocytes, observed in explants treated with 100 ng/mL
IGF-1, were the manifestation of a of a hypertrophic phenotype, the collagen X formation can be
measured by PRO-C10 ELISA assay. Here, the antibody detects the neo-epitope, SFSGFLVAPM, at
the C-terminal of collagen X [127].
Fourthly, the next step after examining the additive eﬀects of IGF-1 and dynamic compression
in a translational ex vivo bovine cartilage model, is to test IGF-1 and dynamic compression in a
translational ex vivo OA human cartilage model.
Finally, this demonstrated additive eﬀect of combining biochemical and biomechanical stimuli,
may be of interest for pharmaceutical companies with a potential OA drug. Mechanical loading
may inﬂuence the eﬀect of the drug, which can both be in a positive or a negative way. Thus,
this information may be crucial for pharmaceutical companies when deciding to move forward
potential OA drugs from the preclinical phase to clinical trials.

Protocol 
 
Procedure 
1. Full-depth cartilage (FDC) explants are punched (3mm) from bovine cartilage retrieved from the local 
slaughterhouse max. 24 hrs after slaughtering. 
1.1. The FDC explants are punched out and immediately put in medium in a 50mL falcon tube with culture 
medium. 
1.2. The FDC explants are brought to the cell culture room. 
 
2. The explants are washed 3 times in medium, plated into a 96well plate according to the plate setup and 
cultured in 200 µL culture media. 
 
3. Supernatants are harvested and treatments are added according to the experimental set-up. 
3.1. The treatment-media are mixed according to the calculation. 
3.2. The supernatant from each plate is transferred to a 96-well plate labeled with exp. name, date, initials, 
plate # and supernatant #, and stored in –18 C. 
3.3. 200 µL treatment-media is added to all plates according to the plate set-up.  
 
4. Each Friday of the experiment, the explants are measured by the Alamar Blue assay 
4.1. Make a solution of culture media with 10% Alamar Blue (Biosource, Dal 1100). (1,5 ml Alamar blue + 
13,5 ml medium culture) 
4.2. Harvest the last supernatant as described in 3. 
4.3. Add 200 µL 10% Alamar Blue solution to each well with cartilage and 4 empty wells (for background). 
Incubate 3 hours at 37C (where the explants are normally incubated). It is important to stop the 
incubation, when there are still differences between the wells (they will all turn pink eventually).  
4.4. Transfer 160 µL to a black 96-well plate, NO BUBBLES… and read the plate (Fluorescence, EX: 540 EM: 
590). 
4.5. Wash the cartilage 3 times in medium. And refill with PBS in the 4 empty wells (A1-A4)  
  
5. Continue culturing 
5.1. The treatment-media are mixed according to the calculation. 
5.2. The supernatant from each plate is transferred to a 96-well plate labeled with exp. name, date, initials, 
plate # and supernatant #, and stored in –20 C. 
5.3. 200 µL treatment-media is added to all plates according to the plate set-up.  
 
6. The explants are fixed in new plates. 
6.1. Explants are transferred to new plates.  
6.2. 200 µL formaldehyde 4% is added to all explants. 
6.3. Change the formaldehyde to PBS after 2 hours and store at 4 degrees.  
6.4. The explants are now ready for histological preparation.  
 
7. The supernatant retrieved from the experiment are ready for biomarker analysis and stored at –20 C.

AlamarBlue assay 
 
 
Introduction 
The AlamarBlue assay is designed to measure the proliferation of cells. The AlamarBlue assay works as a 
fluorometric/colorimetric growth indicator based on detection of metabolic activity. The system is an oxida-
tion-reduction indicator that changes fluorescence and colour according to reduction, which results from cell 
growth. Reduction related to growth causes the oxidation-reduction indicator to change from an oxidized, 
non-fluorescent form (blue) to a reduced, fluorescent form (red). 
 
 
Chemicals: 
AlamarBlue, Invitrogen, Cat. No.: DAL1100 
 
 
Reagents: 
10% AlamarBlue solution: i.e. 1 ml AlamarBlue + 9 ml -MEM 
 
 
Equipment: 
Spectra Max M5 
 
 
Assay procedure: 
 
1. Remove the supernatant from the cells and transfer it to a new 96 well plate. Often the supernatants 
are going to be used for Calcium or CTX release. Store at -20˚C. 
 
2. Add carefully 200 µl 10% Alamar Blue to each well with cells and to two wells without cells as con-
trols and for background measurement. 
 
3. Incubate in 37C/ 5% CO2 and control the colour change every hour in the beginning.  
Control the plate more often when the colour change is almost as wanted. The colour will change 
from blue to purple when the cells are alive.  
 
4. When the AlamarBlue solution is purple transfer the medium to a black well plate with black bottom. 
Avoid bobbles!!! 
 
5. Fluorescence measurement with SpectraMax of the AlamarBlue solution.  
Excitation 540 nm and emission 590 nm

SUMMARY AND EXPLANATION  
C2M ELISA is for research use only, and is an in-vitro di-
agnostic device intended for the quantitative determi-
nation of matrix metalloproteinase (MMP) generated 
fragments of type II collagen (C2M) in human serum. 
Data suggest that C2M ELISA can be used as an aid in 
the evaluation of tissue injury in patients with Rheuma-
toid Arthritis in conjunction with other laboratory and 
clinical findings. 
C2M 
ELISA 
is 
detecting 
peptide 
fragments 
GPPGRDGAAG generated by proteolytic cleavage of 
type II collagen. 
 
3 
PRINCIPLE OF THE ASSAY 
In the initiating step, the Biotinylated Antigen 
(KGPPGRDGAAG) is bound to Streptavidin-coated mi-
crotiter wells. 
After pre-incubation, the wells are emptied and washed. 
Standard, Controls, or unknown sample(s) in appropri-
ate dilutions are pipetted into the wells preferably in 
triple determination for controls and samples, followed 
by addition of Horseradish-Peroxidase conjugated Anti-
body (HRP-AB). 
After incubation, the wells are emptied and washed fol-
lowed by addition of Substrate Solution (TMB Sens).  
The color reaction is stopped by addition of Sulphuric 
Acid.  
The absorbance is measured at 450 nm with 650 nm as 
reference.  
The concentration is calculated using a 4-parametric 
curve fit model.

Handle and dispose all specimens using Universal Pre-

Human serum can be used for the C2M tests.

below for up to 48 hours and are stable for 3 times 
freeze/thaw cycles. 
 
7 
ASSAY PROCEDURE 
For optimal performance of the assay it is important 
to comply with the instructions given below 
Prior to use, prepare and equilibrate all solutions to 
room temperature. Perform the assay at room temper-
ature (18-22°C). Mix all reagents and samples before 
use. Avoid formation of foam. 
Determine the number of strips needed for the assay. 
It is recommended to test all samples in triplicates using 
22 wells for the standards and controls on each ELISA 
plate. Place the appropriate number of strips in the 
plastic frame.   
1. Preparation of Biotinylated Antigen Solution  
Mix the Biotinylated Antigen (Coater) and Coating 
Buffer (1+100) in a 15 mL tube. Mix carefully, avoiding 
formation of foam. 
2. Coating with Biotinylated Antigen Solution 
Add 100 µL of Biotinylated Antigen Solution to each 
well, cover with sealing tape, and incubate for 30±5 
minutes at room temperature (18-22°C) on a microtiter 
plate mixing apparatus (300 rpm). 
3. Washing 
Wash the wells 5 times manually or with an automated 
plate washer with Washing Solution diluted 1+50 in dis-
tilled water. Each well must be filled completely. Tap 
the empty wells firmly against clean paper towels to 
make sure the wells are completely emptied after each 
washing cycle.

8 
QUALITY CONTROL 
Good laboratory practice recommends use of controls 
to ensure that the assay is performing properly. Each 
C2M ELISA kit contains high and low controls that can 
be used for this purpose. Control ranges are provided. 
The control values are intended to verify the validity of 
the curve and sample results. It is recommended for 
each laboratory to include their own in-house control 
preferable with human serum as sample material. Each 
laboratory should establish its own parameters for ac-
ceptable assay limits.  
If the control values are not within your laboratory’s ac-
ceptance limits, the assay results should be repeated. 
For each lot, a Technical datasheet is included and is to 
be used to verify that the results obtained by your la-
boratory are similar to those obtained at Nordic Biosci-
ence Development and Production (NBDP). 
 
9 
INTERPRETATION OF RESULTS 
Calculate the mean of the absorbance determinations. 
Construct a standard curve using a 4-parametric fit 
model with mean absorbance of eight standards A-H 
(ordinate) against the corresponding C2M concentra-
tions (abscissa). The C2M analyte concentration of the 
controls and each sample is determined by interpola-
tion. 
It is recommended to run samples in triplicates: 
- 
If intra-CV ≤ 10%, mean of all three observa-
tions should be used. 
- 
If intra-CV ≥ 10%, the outlier is removed, and 
the mean is calculated from the two observa-
tions.  
Concentrations are given as ng/mL. For the conversion 
to nM use the following equation: nM = (ng/mL) / 0.85 
kDa. 
An example of C2M ELISA standard curve with the four 
parameters A, B, C, and D are depicted in figure 3.

11 PERFORMANCE OF THE TEST 
11.1 STANDARD CURVE 
The half maximal inhibitory concentration (IC50) is de-
termined as being in the range 0.45 – 0.60 ng/mL. 
The optical density (OD) is determined as being in the 
range 1.50 – 2.30 Abs. 
11.2 LIMITS OF DETECTION 
LLOD: The Lower Limit of Detection (LLOD), determined 
as 3SD of the blank has been assessed as 0.02 ng/mL. 
Measurement range: The measurement range is de-
fined as the range between Lower Limit of Quantifica-
tion (LLOQ) and Upper Limit of Quantification (ULOQ). 
This was determined as 0.13-2.00 ng/mL. 
 
11.3 PRECISION 
Precision was determined by assaying three replicates 
of five samples in 10 different runs.  
Inter- and intra assay variations (CV%) were deter-
mined to be in the range 4.50 – 15.00% and 2.00 – 
8.00%, respectively (0.20– 1.30 ng/mL). 
 
11.4 LINEARITY 
Linearity was determined using three samples of hu-
man serum (0.92 – 0.30 ng/mL). C2M was linear with 
relative error 0.00-16.90%.

Safranin O Staining Protocol for Cartilage 
  
  
Description: This method is used for the detection of cartilage, mucin, and mast cell granules on 
formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded tissue sections, and may be used for frozen sections as well. The 
cartilage and mucin will be stained orange to red, and the nuclei will be stained black. The background is 
stained green. 
  
Fixation: Formalin fixed, paraffin embedded sections. 
  
Solutions and Reagents:  
Generally: Solutions are stable for about 1 year. Filtered solutions can be used for 1 month. 
   
Weigert's Iron Hematoxylin Solution:  
   
Stock Solution A:  
Hematoxylin ----------------------------- 1 g  
95% Ethanol ----------------------------- 100 ml  
 
Stock Solution B:  
29% Ferric chloride in water ----------- 4 ml  
Distilled water -------------------------- 95 ml  
Hydrochloric acid, concentrated ---- 1ml  
   
Weigert's Iron Hematoxylin Working Solution:  
Mix equal parts of stock solution A and B. This working solution is stable for about 4 weeks, but needs to 
be freshly filtered prior to use.  
    
1% HCL ethanol solution:  
Hydrochloric acid, concentrated ----------- 1 ml  
70% ethanol -------------------------------- 100 ml  
 
0.05% Fast Green (FCF) Solution:  
Fast green, FCF, C.I. 42053 ------------- 0.5 g  
Distilled water ---------------------------- 1000 ml  
   
1% Acetic Acid Solution:  
Acetic acid, glacial ------------------------- 1 ml  
Distilled water ------------------------------ 99 ml  
   
0.1% Safranin O Solution:  
Safranin O, C.I. 50240 -------------------- 0.1 g  
Distilled water ----------------------------- 100 ml

Procedure:  
   
1. Melt slides at 60°C incubator for 1 hr. 
2. Prepare and filter all reagents. 
3. Deparaffinize slides. 
a. 
Toluene 10 min., toluene 10 min., 99% ethanol 2 min., 99% ethanol 2 min., 96% ethanol 
2 min., 96% ethanol 2 min., 70% ethanol 2 min., 70% ethanol 2 min. 
4. Hydrate slides. 
a. 
 Milli-Q water 2 min. 
5. Stain with Weigert’s iron hematoxylin working solution for 10 minutes.  
6. Dip 1 time in 1% HCL ethanol solution. 
7. Wash in running tap water until excess color is gone.  
8. Stain with 0.05% fast green (FCF) solution for 5 minutes.  
9. Dip 1 time in 1% acetic acid solution.  
10. Stain in 0.1% safranin O solution for 20 minutes.  
11. Dehydrate and clear slides. 
a. 
70% 1 dip, 70% 1 dip, 96% ethanol 2 min., 96% ethanol 2 min., 99% ethanol 2 min., 99% 
ethanol 2 min., toluene 2 min., toluene 2 min. 
12. Mount using resinous medium (white tube). 
   
Results:  
   
     Nuclei ---------------------------------------------- black  
     Cytoplasm ----------------------------------------- gray green  
     Cartilage, mucin, mast cell granules ----------- orange to red
