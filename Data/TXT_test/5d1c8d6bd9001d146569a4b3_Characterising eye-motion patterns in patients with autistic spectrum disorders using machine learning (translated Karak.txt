Abstract
Do individuals with Autism Spectrum Disorder (ASD) look at faces diﬀerently
from individuals without ASD? Several studies have investigated this, however
the results are varying. This is due to diﬀerent experimental setups and perhaps
that they used diﬀerent predeﬁned areas of interest (AOIs) in the face and merely
counted how long the subjects looked inside them.
In this thesis, ﬁrst-order
Hidden Markov Models (HMMs) with Gaussian (mixture) emissions and repeated
stratiﬁed K-fold cross validation were instead used to model the face viewing
patterns of individuals with and without ASD. The purpose was to construct a
(Bayes) classiﬁer that could diagnose ASD solely based upon the eye tracking
data obtained by Johnels et al. 2017, where subjects with and without ASD
looked at short movies and images of faces showing either no emotion (neutral)
or an emotion (anger, fear and happiness).
When using the data obtained from looking at the images of faces with anger,
fear or happiness, the classiﬁer turned out signiﬁcantly diﬀerent from random
to correctly classify 6-7 out of 10 on average, whereas for neutral and all four
combined it was 5-6 out of 10 correctly classiﬁed and not signiﬁcantly diﬀerent
from random. The performance was best using only the happy faces with a mean
area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) being 0.657. This
agrees with studies concluding a diﬀerence in viewing patterns as a function of
facial emotions compared to neutral and also the study of Johnels et al. 2017
which expected and found diﬀerences speciﬁc to the happy faces.
Further studies could attempt to improve the classiﬁer, for example by tailor-
ing the HMM towards the speciﬁc problem, by learning more about the ASD and
control groups from other features or methods and use it in the classiﬁer, by at-
tempting to use the dynamic videos instead of static pictures, and ﬁnally improve
the quantity and quality of the data by redoing the eye tracking experiment.

Introduction
Autism spectrum disorder (ASD) is a developmental disorder. Among other diﬃ-
culties, individuals with ASD have diﬃculty with communication and interaction
with other people. As an example they make little or inconsistent eye contact
and tend not to look at or listen to people [NIH 2018].
Communication with and understanding the emotions of the people we interact
with on a daily basis is one of the most important aspects of having a good so-
cial life and being able to interact successfully with other people. Whether it is
marriage or modern project management, being able to understand people and
their emotions is very crucial. This is of course a problem for people with ASD,
since the face is the primary way of reading emotions.
Johnels et al. 2017 recently did a study on ASD. In their research article, they
mention that in the ASD research ﬁeld there is a notion that compared to others,
individuals with ASD look more towards the mouths instead of eyes. However,
they then cite several studies on the matter and mention that when over a dozen
studies are reviewed, only two studies show this pattern of increased mouth-
viewing. They mention that a reason for the conﬂicting studies could be that
face-viewing patterns may change from childhood to adulthood which was not
taking into account in the studies. Another reason could be that pictures of faces
without emotions were used instead of faces with emotions. To support this they
cite studies that have shown that the mouth and lower part of the face seem to be
important for identifying happiness and to some extent anger and that the eyes
seem to be used for identifying sadness. A third reason could be that the images
were static instead of dynamic videos. Static pictures instead of ﬁlm stimuli may
not be good enough to reveal subtle diﬀerences in how the subjects are looking at
diﬀerent parts of the face as a function of characteristic changes for the diﬀerent
emotions.
Johnels et al. 2017 are only aware of a few eye-tracking studies using pictures or
videos with several emotional expressions for individuals with ASD. They ﬁrst
cite a study of viewing videos of faces transitioning from a neutral expression to
an emotion. It showed for the 28 children with ASD in the study that they were
slower in recognizing emotion compared to others [Bal et al. 2010]. However,
this study did not reveal that any group looked more towards the eyes or mouth.
Another study found that viewing images of faces expressing diﬀerent emotions
only showed few diﬀerences between the ASD group and the control (CON) group
in looking at the eyes versus the mouth. However, the ASD-participants in this
study looked generally less at complex facial expressions (e.g. arrogant) versus
4
less complex (e.g., fear), and they seemed to look less towards the eyes for these
stimuli, however the diﬀerence was not signiﬁcant. A third study used all six ba-
sic emotions (fear, anger, disgust, happiness, sadness, and surprise). They found
diﬀerences between the ASD and CON subjects in how much they looked at eyes
and mouth, though only for some of the emotions [Corden, Chilvers, and Skuse
2008]. A fourth study looked at calm and happy faces versus angry and fearful
faces in a small group of children with ASD. They reported that both groups
diﬀerentiated between the calm/happy faces and the angry/fearful faces. Both
groups looked less at the eyes for calm/happy faces than for angry/fearful faces
[de-Wit, Falck-Ytter, and Hofsten 2008], but they did not formally analyze if it
was the mouth that was looked more towards.
In their own study, Johnels et al. 2017 used infrared eye-tracking technology to
investigate the face-viewing patterns of 57 subjects with ASD and 58 without,
while they looked at dynamic emotional facial expressions. They sub-grouped
children and adolescents (< 18 years) in one group and adults (> 18 years) in
another and investigated diagnostic and age-related eﬀects. In particular, they
compared viewing patterns towards the mouth and the eyes for diﬀerent dynamic
emotional facial expressions (neutral, angry, happy, and fear). Due to the mixed
previous results, they did not have strong predictions of overall group diﬀerences
in how much was looked towards the eyes or mouth. However, they did expect
that the ASD subjects would be less diﬀerentiated in how they look depending
on the emotional expression of the face and that they would fail to show a typical
orientation towards the mouth of happy faces.
They did not ﬁnd any support to the hypothesis of more mouth viewing in autism
which agrees with the majority of studies in the ﬁeld. They found that gaze
behaviour is aﬀected by the emotional content of the face, and that this behaviour
is not fully developed in children with ASD, and speciﬁc for the two groups (ASD
and CON), they found the following:
1. With regards to the children, there were signiﬁcant diﬀerences between the
ASD and the control group for the angry and the happy face conditions,
but not for neutral and fear. For angry faces, the ASD group showed lower
ﬁxation times on the mouth compared with the control group, but there was
no diﬀerence for the eyes. For happy faces, the ASD group again showed
lower ﬁxation times on the mouth and more ﬁxation times on the eyes
compared to the control group. In addition, the expected increase in looking
towards mouths of happy faces compared to other expressions seemed to
be present in both groups but relatively less in the ASD group. They also
found that with increasing autism-like traits in the control group, measured
5
by the Autism Quotient (AQ), there was looked relatively less towards the
happy mouth. So, the same observed pattern in the children/adolescent
ASD group was also seen in the control children/adolescents with higher
AQ scores.
2. With regards to the adults, they observed only a few diﬀerences between
the two groups. Their main result was that the ASD subjects looked less
towards the eyes of neutral faces. Both groups seemed also to look equally
more towards the mouth of happy faces compared to the mouth of the other
emotions, except the ASD group who looked equally much at the angry and
happy mouth. Lastly, viewing times on the mouth of angry, neutral and
fearful faces were the same in both groups.
When they ﬁnally compared all ASD and control participants they did not ﬁnd
any signiﬁcant diﬀerences in eye and mouth viewing for any emotion. However,
in the ﬁnal comparison they again saw that the control children with increas-
ing autism-like traits focused less on the mouth of happy faces compared to the
mouth of neutral faces.
The ﬁve, mentioned studies [Johnels et al. 2017; Bal et al. 2010; Rutherford and
Towns 2008; Corden, Chilvers, and Skuse 2008; de-Wit, Falck-Ytter, and Hofsten
2008] have in common that they obtained somewhat diﬀerent results and used
predeﬁned regions/areas of interest (ROIs/AOIs) in which they merely counted
how much was looked (as mentioned in their method sections). Diﬀerent experi-
mental setups of course plays a role in these diﬀerences, but perhaps a new way
forward is to remove the assumptions of AOIs, because how to deﬁne them? The
results would directly depend on the choice of AOIs and their sizes. Instead, one
could make use of mathematical models and machine learning techniques which
could learn these areas. For example, it is reasonable that the hidden states of
a Hidden Markov Model (HMM) would correspond to the AOIs which one study
also alludes to [Brueggemann, Chan, and Hsiao 2016], and HMMs are already
being used in other areas with eye tracking data as well, for example general
behaviour [Coutrot and Antoni B. Chan 2017; Kim et al. 2018].
This thesis will thus try to contribute to the aforementioned studies by trying
to develop a general, mathematical model for the viewing patterns of ASD and
control subjects in order make a diagnose tool that can determine whether a
subject has ASD or not. The data comes from the study of Johnels et al. 2017
and HMMs will be used for the modelling.

The data
5.1
Experimental setup
Johnels et al. 2017 recruited participants with ASD through clinics and autism
associations and the control group was recruited through advertisement. The
participants performed the experiment in a quiet room with constant lighting.
Participants were seated and placed approximately 60 cm from the Tobii T120
eye-tracking monitor. Participants were shown a series of 32 short movies cre-
ated from the NimStim database [Tottenham et al. 2009]. Meanwhile their eye
movements were recorded. Each movie was a 3 seconds dynamic morph starting
from a neutral face transitioning into either angry, fear or happy. Then, the ﬁnal
expression in the video was shown as a static photo for another 2 seconds. The
participants could look freely at the screen and was told to merely look at the
faces [Johnels et al. 2017].

Preprocessing
I received 137 tab-separated ﬁles (.tsv). Each ﬁle consists of the exported data
from a subject’s eye tracking experiment with the Tobii software. However, there
are only 133 diﬀerent subjects since four of them have two ﬁles. The data in these
pairs are not the same, hence it seems that these four subjects did the experiment
twice. Of the 133 diﬀerent subjects, 66 had ASD and 67 were control subjects.

corresponding experiments was approximately twice as fast. In addition, two

experiment because the ﬁrst was stopped. These two ﬁles with fewer observations
will thus be removed before the modelling and their later ﬁles are used. The ﬁrst
ﬁles of the other two subjects with double ﬁles will also be disregarded and their

The modelling
6.1
Hidden Markov Models
A Hidden Markov Model (HMM) can be used to model sequential data and
is often used in speech recognition, natural language modelling, on-line hand-
writing recognition and analysis of biological sequences such as proteins and
DNA [Bishop 2006 pp. 610]. In a HMM it is assumed that the data sequence
X = [x1, x2, ..., xN] we observe is generated by a sequence of some underlying
latent variables Z = [z1, z2, ..., zN] that can be in diﬀerent (therefore hidden)
states which cannot be directly observed, consider Figure 9.
Figure 9: Sequential data and Markov chain of latent variables with each
observation conditioned on the state of the corresponding latent variable
[Taken from Bishop 2006 pp. 609].
In addition, in each (often time) step, the model either changes state or remains
in the same state according to some probability distribution collected in the tran-
sition probability matrix A with elements called transition probabilities. These
are given by Ajk = p(znk = 1|zn−1,j = 1) i.e. the probability of entering state
k at step n when at step n −1 we was in state j. Consider Figure 10 for an
illustration of the transition probabilities.
17
Figure 10: Transition diagram showing a model whose latent variables
have three possible states corresponding to the three boxes. The black
lines denote the elements of the transition matrix Ajk [Taken from Bishop
2006 pp. 611].
The transitions between hidden states follow a Markov chain, which means that
the transition distribution only depends on the current state of the system for a
ﬁrst-order Markov chain, hence p(zn|zn−1), and only depends on the two most
recent states for a second-order Markov chain, hence p(zn|zn−1, zn−2) and so on.

We can look at three diﬀerent problems for HMMs [hmmlearn 2018]:
1. Training: Given some observations X, estimate the model parameters θ =
{π, A, φ}. In the example, assume you return from a travel and she talks
about how her moods have been each day during the week. From only this
information, you must calculate the diﬀerent probabilities. This problem
can be solved by the Expectation-Maximization (EM) algorithm.

2. Recognition: Given the model parameters θ and observations X, estimate
the optimal sequence of hidden states. In the example, if you only have her
daily moods, estimate how each work day has been. This problem can be
solved by the Viterbi algorithm.
3. Evaluation: Given the model parameters θ and observations X, calculate
the likelihood of the data p(X|θ) i.e. the probability of the observation
sequence given the model. This problem can be solved by the Forward-
Backward algorithm.

6.1.2
hmmlearn in Python
hmmlearn is a package in Python that implements Hidden Markov Models [hmm-
learn 2018]. There are three build-in models:
1. With Gaussian emissions
2. With Gaussian mixture emissions
3. With Multinomial (discrete) emissions
It is also possible to implement a custom emission probability.
Some of the
parameters the model with normal Gaussian emissions can take are the following
1. n components (int) – Number of hidden states
2. covariance type (string) - Which can be:
24
(a) ”full” - When the HMM is ergodic (fully connected) i.e. each state
can be reached from any other state in ﬁnite steps. Each state has its
own general/full covariance matrix.
(b) ”diag” - When each state uses its own diagonal covariance matrix. The
contour axes are oriented along the coordinate axes
(c) ”spherical” - Each state uses a single variance value that applies to
all features i.e.
a diagonal covariance matrix with the same value
in the diagonal. The shape is circular in 2D and spherical in higher
dimensions
(d) ”tied” - When all states share the same general/full covariance matrix,
so the distributions have the same shape
3. n iter (int) – Maximum number of iterations to perform
4. tol (ﬂoat) – Convergence threshold. EM will stop if the gain in log-likelihood
is below this value.
5. params (string) – Controls which parameters are updated in the training
process. Can contain any combination of ‘s’ for startprob, ‘t’ for transmat,
‘m’ for means and ‘c’ for covars. Defaults to all parameters.
6. init params (string) – Controls which parameters are initialized prior to
training. Can contain any combination of ‘s’ for startprob, ‘t’ for transmat,
‘m’ for means and ‘c’ for covars. Defaults to all parameters.
The Gaussian HMM also have the following functions:
1. decode: Finds the most likely state sequence corresponding to X and the
log-likelihood of the produced state sequence (recognition and evaluation)
2. fit(X, lengths): Estimates the model parameters by the Baum-Welch
algorithm. Gives the option to pass proper init_params to avoid the ini-
tialization step before the EM algorithm starts. Multiple sequences can be
concatenated in X and the lengths of each sequence are then passed in the
vector lengths (training)
3. predict: Finds the most likely state sequence corresponding to X (recog-
nition)
4. predict_proba: Computes the posterior probability for each state in the
model i.e. the state-membership probabilities for each sample from X. So
for each observation in X, a probability for being in each state is returned
25
5. sample: Generate random samples (emissions) from the model and the
corresponding state sequence produced by the model
6. score(X, lengths=None): Computes the log-likelihood of X. Multiple
sequences can be concatenated in X and the lengths of each sequence are
then passed in the vector lengths (evaluation)
7. score_samples: Computes the log-likelihood of X and the the state-membership
probabilities for each sample from X (evaluation)
6.2
Creating the classiﬁer
The emissions/observations in the HMM are the ﬁxation points, consider Figure
8 from earlier as an example. It is assumed that these emissions are generated
by some hidden states of ﬁxations and that the transitions between these follow
a ﬁrst-order Markov chain. The distribution of the emissions is assumed to be
Gaussian. The covariance matrix is chosen to be full or ergodic such that each
hidden state can be reached from any other hidden state (the participants were
free to look anywhere on the screen). By ﬁtting one HMM to the ASD sequences
and another HMM to the CON sequences, the hypothesis is that, by calculating
the corresponding (log) likelihood from each model on the data of a new test
subject, we can use these two likelihoods in a Bayes classiﬁer that can classify
whether the new test subject has ASD or not.
Now, in order to train a HMM model on the data and to estimate its performance,
two layer K-fold cross validation is performed. In the inner cross validation, the
purpose is to ﬁnd the best model order i.e. the number of hidden states in the
HMM. In the outer cross validation, the purpose is to ﬁnd an unbiased estimate
for the performance of that model [Herlau, Schmidt, and Mørup 2018 pp. 133-
135]. In addition, the cross validation will also be stratiﬁed such that we maintain
the same ratio of the two classes (ASD and CON) in the splits. This is needed
since we have to create two models, one for each class, and a Bayes classiﬁer. See
Figure 15 for an overview of the cross validation, which are then expanded on
below.
26
Figure 15: An overview of the two-layer cross validation that are used to
train and evaluate the model used to predict ASD versus CON.
In the inner cross validation, the best model for each class was to be found. Be-
sides the mean and variance for the distribution of each hidden state, which the
EM algorithm takes care of, we need to ﬁnd the model order (the number of
of hidden states K) that maximizes the inner test log-likelihood (or the score).
Kinner-fold cross validation is selected in order to determine the model order.
First, diﬀerent model orders are selected, for example Kinner = [5, 10, ..., 50]. For
each of these orders, we train a HMM model with Gaussian emissions on the
Kinner −1 inner training folds. The HMM is trained upon all the combined ﬁxa-
tion point sequences of these inner ASD train subjects. We thus regard all their
sequences as one big population. The score is then calculated on the inner test
data for each model. Here, we also regard all the test subject’s sequences as one
population. When all inner folds have been used for training and testing, we then
sum the scores for each model and select the model with the highest sum. The
result of the inner cross validation is therefore only these two model orders, for
example KASD = 45 and KCON = 50.
Another possibility instead of selecting the highest scored model in the inner cross
validation is to predict ASD versus CON in the inner cross validation as well.
This way, we can select the best model order K using both the information of
when the likelihood is maximized and which K that predicts the best. In order
to make this prediction, we will construct a Bayes classiﬁer. Firstly, we need the

then adding 1 to ea in the classiﬁer ruins it and 1+ea becomes 1 and the classiﬁer
therefore becomes 1 as well. Even when a is not very positive or negative, we are
still not without problems. Many of the corresponding classiﬁer values are then

and 1 in order to calculate the AUC since the important things takes place close

Discussion
Individuals with ASD tend to make little or inconsistent eye contact and also
completely ignore looking at other people’s faces. But when they do look, is the
viewing pattern then considerably diﬀerent from others? Many studies have in-
vestigated this, one of the latest being Johnels et al. 2017, however they obtained
a bit varying results. This is caused by diﬀerent experimental setups and perhaps
that they merely counted how long the subjects looked at diﬀerent predeﬁned ar-
eas of interest in the face using eye-tracking software and then did some statistical
analyses. In this thesis, another approach was taken. Using ﬁrst-order Hidden
Markov Models (HMMs) with Gaussian emissions and repeated stratiﬁed K-fold
cross validation, models for the face viewing patterns of experimental subjects
with and without ASD were developed using the eye tracking data from the study
of Johnels et al. 2017. The purpose was to construct a tool that could diagnose
ASD based on a Bayes classiﬁer.
On average, the classiﬁer turned out to correctly classify 6-7 out of 10 with
an area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) being 0.657.
When manually displacing the ﬁxation points in the CON sequences even by
just +10 pixels in the x- and y-direction, the classiﬁer became better, and the
more displacement, the better classiﬁer. This seems to show that the classiﬁer
is constructed correctly, hence the results indicate that a ﬁrst-order HMM with
Gaussian (ﬁxation point) emissions is not strong enough to clearly model diﬀer-
ences in the viewing patterns between the ASD and control subjects. However,
there seems to be a signiﬁcant diﬀerence across emotions in the predictions. The
ten repetitions of 10-fold cross validation shown in Table 3 let to a mean AUC of
0.618, 0.630 and 0.678 for the angry, fear and happy faces, respectively, which are
all signiﬁcant better than random (p = 3.1%, p = 1.9% and p = 0.2%), whereas
for the neutral and combined faces, the mean AUCs were 0.559 and 0.558 which
were both not signiﬁcant (p = 16.9% and p = 16.40%). In addition, when per-
muting the class labels before the modelling and performing the computations
again several times, the AUCs drop towards 0.5 and all become non-signiﬁcant.
A permutation test of course needs many more repetitions, however the few per-
formed still seem to indicate that the obtained results are indeed signiﬁcant. It
is thus plausible to conclude that the classiﬁer performs better when emotions
are involved compared to neutral. Hence, when there are no emotions involved,
the viewing patterns between the ASD and control subjects are more alike and
the classiﬁer cannot distinguish them. This agrees with the studies of Johnels
et al. 2017; Bal et al. 2010; Rutherford and Towns 2008; Corden, Chilvers, and
Skuse 2008; de-Wit, Falck-Ytter, and Hofsten 2008], which showed that there is a
diﬀerence in viewing patterns as a function of facial emotions. Johnels et al. 2017
also expected the ASD participants to fail to show the typical orientation toward
45
the mouth of happy faces, and they also found several diﬀerences explicitly re-
garding happy faces. This is perhaps mirrored in this thesis, since the classiﬁer
performs best when using just the happy faces for the number of hidden states
K = 9. Even though higher K’s increased the log-likelihood of the data, they
did not predict better.
A classiﬁer that only classiﬁes 6-7 out of 10 correctly is of course in need of
improvement. Further studies could attempt to improve the HMM, for example
by looking more at Gaussian mixture emissions or other emission distributions
since the assumption that the emissions are Gaussian might not hold.
They
could also increase the order of the Markov chain or look at other covariance
matrices for the Gaussian distribution. And even though we know the classes
(ASD or CON) beforehand, which makes it supervised learning, the approach of
this thesis also leans towards unsupervised learning in the sense that the HMMs
are ﬁtted using only the ﬁxation points as the data matrix X. Other features
from the received data ﬁles were also added, however without obtaining better
results, but the other features should be examined further. In addition, instead
of using a (limited) HMM package, implementing the EM algorithm described in
subsection 6.1.1 personally would perhaps also allow for the model to be more
tailored to the speciﬁc problem. If the EM algorithm was implemented personally,
one could perhaps make a transition matrix speciﬁc for each subject and use it
as an additional feature vector.
Other methods such as principal component
analysis might also be utilized in order to learn more about the classes and
obtain additional feature vectors. Johnels et al. 2017 also believes that better
results are obtained when using dynamic video stimuli instead of static pictures.
I also attempted to use the videos, however it did not lead to better results. But
this could be examined additionally as well, the HMMs might need to be more
tailored.
The quality of the data used in the modelling could also be improved. Firstly,
there were many missing values (the unclassiﬁed gaze events).
For the ASD
subjects, approximately 2/3 of all ﬁxation point sequences had missing values
and for CON, approximately 1/2. Some sequences were even completely empty.
Secondly, even though the pictures of the faces were of same size, there are some
(minor) diﬀerences in where the eyes, noses and mouths are placed in the images.
Since we pool all the sequences, this would aﬀect the model, for example the
mean of the (Gaussian) hidden states.
Lastly, even though the classiﬁer should not be implemented at all hospitals
across the globe immediately, this thesis might contribute to the ASD area and
other areas where eye tracking data is applicable by having shown that it indeed
seems plausible for hidden markov models to be used with eye tracking data,
the models just might need to be a little bit more complex than ﬁrst-order with
Gaussian emissions and only two features.
